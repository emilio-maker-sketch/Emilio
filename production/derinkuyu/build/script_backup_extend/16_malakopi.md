# MALAKOPI

For most of its later history, the town above Derinkuyu was not called Derinkuyu at all. Its Greek-speaking Christian inhabitants called it Malakopi. Derinkuyu is Turkish and means deep well.

Cappadocia was home to a community of Orthodox Christians who spoke their own form of Greek, quite different from the Greek of Athens. In the early twentieth century the British scholar Richard Dawkins travelled through their villages, Malakopi among them, and recorded their dialect before it disappeared.

How much the families of Malakopi still used the old passages is not well recorded. The rooms nearest the surface may well have served as cellars and stores, as rock-cut rooms did all over Cappadocia, but the deep levels seem to have slipped out of everyday memory.

Then, in 1923, after the war between Greece and Turkey, the two countries agreed to exchange their populations. Orthodox Christians in Turkey were sent to Greece, and Muslims in Greece were sent to Turkey. More than a million people were moved. The Christians of Malakopi left their homes, and with them went the memory of what lay underneath.

The agreement was signed at Lausanne, in Switzerland, on the thirtieth of January 1923. It did not ask what language people spoke or how they saw themselves. It asked only their religion. So the Karamanlides of Cappadocia, Orthodox Christians whose everyday language was Turkish, were classed as Greeks and sent to Greece, a country many of them had never seen and whose language some of them barely spoke.

The villages emptied. People were allowed to take only what they could carry. Some carried their icons, some the keys of their houses. In Greece, many refugee communities named their new villages after the old ones. Families from Gelveri, today Güzelyurt, about thirty-five kilometres west of Derinkuyu, founded a new village in northern Greece and called it Nea Karvali, New Gelveri.

Muslim families arriving from Greece were settled in the houses they left behind. In the space of a few years, the population of many Cappadocian towns was almost completely replaced. The newcomers had no reason to know what the old cellars led to, and no one was left to tell them.

Forty years later, when a man knocked through his wall, the town above had new people who did not know the passages were there. The city had to be discovered all over again.
