# GOING DOWN

From the street, almost nothing shows. The entrance is a low doorway in a modest building in the middle of town. Inside, a passage slopes down, so narrow and low in places that visitors have to stoop and walk in single file. That was not an accident. A narrow passage is easy to defend.

The first levels are the busiest. Here are the rooms people needed every day: stables for animals near the surface, wide halls, kitchens blackened by smoke, storerooms and niches for lamps. Basins cut into the floor are usually read as wine or oil presses, though no one can say exactly what was pressed in each.

Below them the city keeps going. Stairs and ramps lead from one level to the next, and every one is a little cooler and darker than the last. At least eight levels are known, reaching about eighty-five metres below the surface. Only part of the complex is open to visitors, and some of it has never been fully cleared.

How was it dug? Not with machines, and not with anything exotic. The walls still carry the marks of the tools: the short strokes of picks and adzes, the kind of iron tools a farmer or a stonemason in Cappadocia would have owned. A digger worked forward into the face of the rock, by the light of an oil lamp, loosening the tuff in small pieces.

Every basket of loose rock then had to be carried out, up the passages and stairs, to the surface. Nobody knows where all of it went. It may have been spread on the fields, used as building material, or simply dumped in the valleys. For a city this size, the amount of rock removed is enormous. That is one reason most researchers think it was the work of many generations, not of one.

Deep down is a long room with a vaulted ceiling, often called the missionary school, though the name is a modern guess. Lower still is a cross-shaped church cut into the rock. A church this deep tells us something important about the people who used the city at a later stage of its history: they were Christians.

Moving between levels was deliberately awkward. Stairs turn, passages narrow, and in places a visitor must crouch to pass. An attacker coming down would have to do so one at a time, bent over, in the dark, while the defenders waited on the far side of a stone door. The architecture itself was a weapon.

How long people stayed underground at any one time is not recorded. Most experts think the city was not a permanent home but a refuge, filled when danger came and emptied when it passed. Families would have brought their animals, their grain and their oil, closed the doors behind them, and waited.
