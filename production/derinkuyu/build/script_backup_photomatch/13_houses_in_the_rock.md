# MANSIONS IN THE ROCK

Not everyone in Byzantine Cappadocia lived in a simple village. Along the valleys there are rock-cut complexes of a quite different kind: great carved facades with arches and columns, opening onto courtyards, with halls, kitchens and stables behind them.

At Selime, at the end of the Ihlara valley, an entire cliff was hollowed out into a vast complex with a church, a huge hall with a high carved ceiling, and a kitchen with a chimney cut through the rock. Researchers such as Veronica Kalas have argued that complexes like this were the houses of the local landowning elite, not only monasteries as was once assumed.

These carved mansions show the other side of rock-cut life. The rich could afford grand rooms in the cliffs, with decorated facades that faced the world. The farmers in the villages, with fewer resources, dug down. Both were making the most of the same rock, in the same centuries, under the same threats.
> WS | slow pan | photo | neutral | | Cappadocian village beneath carved cliffs [q: Cappadocia village cliff dwellings]

It is a reminder that the underground cities were not built by a different people or a lost civilisation. They were built by the same Byzantine Cappadocians whose churches, monasteries and houses fill the valleys above. To understand Derinkuyu, you have to see it as part of that whole landscape, the bottom floor of a world carved into stone.
