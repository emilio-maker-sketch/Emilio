# REFUGE FROM THE RAIDS

We are on firmer ground with the period when the city was used most. From the seventh century AD, Cappadocia became a frontier. To the south-east lay the new Islamic caliphate, to the west the Christian Byzantine Empire, and for roughly three centuries armies and raiding parties crossed this plateau again and again.

The raiders came through the passes of the Taurus Mountains, often in summer, seizing animals, crops and captives. A farming village on an open plain could not hold out against them. But a village that could vanish underground, with its animals, its grain and its water, could simply wait until the raiders moved on.

That is why most historians think the underground cities were greatly enlarged in the middle Byzantine period. The Byzantines themselves knew the tactic. Their military writers advised that, when raiders came, the people of the countryside should take shelter in strongholds and leave nothing behind to be taken.

The church in the lower levels belongs to this world. So do the many rock-cut churches and monasteries of the valleys nearby, painted with saints in the same centuries. Above ground, Cappadocia was a Christian Byzantine province. Below ground, it had a second, hidden life for the times when the frontier caught fire.

The danger did not end with the Arab raids. Later centuries brought Turkish conquest, wars between rival rulers, and bandits. Each time, the cities underground could be opened and filled again. Their use did not stop until the twentieth century.
