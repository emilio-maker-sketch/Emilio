# STONE THAT WEARS AWAY

The fairy chimneys are beautiful, but they are also a warning. The same softness that let people carve the rock lets wind, rain and frost carve it too. Every winter, water seeps into cracks, freezes and pushes the rock apart. Every spring, the meltwater carries the loosened grains away.

A chimney survives as long as its cap. The cap is a block of harder rock, often a layer of more resistant volcanic material, that shields the soft tuff beneath it from the rain. When the cap finally falls, the cone below begins to wear down quickly, and in time it disappears.

People have had to live with this. At Zelve, a valley full of carved houses and churches, families lived in the rock until the middle of the twentieth century. In the 1950s the danger of collapse became too great, and the inhabitants were moved to a new village nearby. Zelve is now an open-air museum of empty rooms.

Not every carved hill was so fragile. At Uçhisar, a great crag of tuff rises over the surrounding valleys, honeycombed with rooms and passages. It was a natural fortress, a lookout over the whole region, and from its top on a clear day you can see Mount Erciyes far to the east.

Underground, the rock is protected from all of this. There is no frost, no rain and no wind below the first few metres. That is one reason the underground cities have survived better than many of the carved houses above. Their enemy is not the weather but water seeping through the rock, and the slow weight of the ground overhead.
