# CHAPTER THREE: HOW IT WAS BUILT

So how do you build a city by digging down? Start with what the builders had. They had a rock that is soft when fresh, and which a person can cut with hand tools. They had iron picks and adzes. They had oil lamps, baskets and rope. And they had time: not years, but generations. That turns out to be enough.

How was it dug? Not with machines, and not with anything exotic. The walls still carry the marks of the tools: the short strokes of picks and adzes, the kind of iron tools a farmer or a stonemason in Cappadocia would have owned. A digger worked forward into the face of the rock, by the light of an oil lamp, loosening the tuff in small pieces.

Every basket of loose rock then had to be carried out, up the passages and stairs, to the surface. Nobody knows where all of it went. It may have been spread on the fields, used as building material, or simply dumped in the valleys. For a city this size, the amount of rock removed is enormous. That is one reason most researchers think it was the work of many generations, not of one.

A city like this is not dug all at once. It probably started with a few rooms, perhaps cellars under houses, reached by short passages. When that room is not enough, the diggers cut another beside it, or a stair leading down to a new room below. Each new level is reached from the one above. Over the generations, a few rooms become a level, and the levels become a city.

Why has it lasted? Tuff is weak compared with granite or limestone. A space cut into it stays up only if its span is modest and enough solid rock is left around it. That is what the builders did. They kept most rooms small, left thick walls and pillars of rock between them, and did not stack every room directly on top of the one below.

The vertical shafts were part of the plan from early on. A level cut deep under the ground is useless if no air reaches it, so shafts had to be dug down from the surface, or up from below, to carry fresh air and to reach water. We will come back to how well that system worked. And the famous rolling stone doors were cut on the spot, from the rock of the city itself, because they could never have been carried down those narrow passages.

So the answer to the first question is simpler than the legends and, in its way, more impressive. Derinkuyu was built by hand, with ordinary iron tools, by lamplight, by people who understood one kind of rock better than almost anyone has before or since, working room by room over many generations. What we cannot say is exactly when it began, how long it took, or how many hands it needed. No record of the work survives, and anyone who gives you precise numbers is guessing.
