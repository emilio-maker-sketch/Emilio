# CHAPTER FOUR: THE MERCHANTS OF KANESH

Long before anyone dug Derinkuyu, Cappadocia was already one of the busiest places in the ancient world. Nearly a hundred kilometres north-east of Derinkuyu, near the modern city of Kayseri, stands a great mound called Kültepe. Under it lie the ruins of a city that its own people called Kanesh.

Nearly four thousand years ago, in the twentieth and nineteenth centuries before Christ, merchants from the city of Assur, in what is now northern Iraq, settled at Kanesh. They lived in their own quarter at the foot of the citadel, the karum, or harbour, although there was no sea. Donkey caravans brought them tin and fine woollen cloth from Mesopotamia, which they sold for silver and gold.

We know this in astonishing detail because the merchants wrote everything down. More than twenty thousand clay tablets have been excavated at Kültepe: letters between business partners, contracts, loans, lawsuits, and letters from wives in Assur complaining that the money had not arrived. They are written in Old Assyrian, in cuneiform, and they form one of the oldest large archives of private letters ever found.

The letters are often startlingly human. Merchants complain about prices and taxes, worry about smugglers on the road, and argue with their partners. Women in Assur ran the weaving side of the business and wrote to their husbands and brothers in Kanesh, sometimes sharply, about money they were owed. Some merchants married local women in Anatolia, and their contracts survive too.

The tablets also preserve something else: the names of the local people. Many of them are in an Indo-European language, the earliest trace of the language we call Hittite. Later the Hittites themselves would call their own tongue nesili, the language of Nesa, which was another name for Kanesh. In a sense, the written history of Anatolia begins here, in Cappadocia.

The Hittite kingdom that followed ruled central Anatolia for centuries, from its capital at Hattusa, north-west of Cappadocia. It fought Egypt at the battle of Kadesh and signed one of the earliest peace treaties that survives. When it collapsed, around 1200 BC, the region broke into smaller kingdoms, and for a while the written record grows thin.

None of this tells us when Derinkuyu was begun. No tablet mentions an underground city, and nothing found in Derinkuyu has been dated to the Bronze Age. But it shows what kind of land this was: rich, crossed by trade routes, and worth fighting over. A land like that would always need places to hide.
