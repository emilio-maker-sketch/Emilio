# CHAPTER FIVE: KATPATUKA

The name Cappadocia first appears in writing in the inscriptions of the Persian king Darius the Great, around 520 BC, as Katpatuka, one of the lands of his empire. You will often read that the name means the land of beautiful horses. It is a lovely story, but its origin is really unknown. What is certain is that Cappadocian horses were famous. The geographer Strabo says the province once paid the Persian king a yearly tribute that included fifteen hundred horses.

The Persian empire fell to Alexander the Great in the 330s BC, but Cappadocia was never fully conquered by him. After his death, a local dynasty took the title of king. The kings of Cappadocia ruled from the city of Mazaca, at the foot of Mount Erciyes, and minted silver coins with their portraits.

Squeezed between larger powers, the kings of Cappadocia learned to survive by choosing their friends carefully. In the end they became clients of Rome. The last king, Archelaus, renamed Mazaca Caesarea in honour of the emperor Augustus, and in the year 17 AD the emperor Tiberius made Cappadocia a Roman province.

Rome's interest was not only strategic. Cappadocia was a land of great estates, horses and flocks, and of temple-states where whole territories belonged to a god and were run by his priests. The most famous was Comana, whose temple of the goddess Ma, Strabo says, had thousands of temple servants. It was a very different world from the cities of the Aegean coast.

Under Rome, the great roads to the eastern frontier ran across the plateau, and Caesarea grew into one of the important cities of Asia Minor. Soldiers and supplies moved east along these roads. So, in later centuries, would invaders moving west.

Christianity arrived early. When the New Testament describes the crowd in Jerusalem at Pentecost, Cappadocians are named among them. The First Letter of Peter is addressed to Christians scattered across Asia Minor, Cappadocia among them. By the fourth century, Caesarea was one of the great centres of the Christian world.
