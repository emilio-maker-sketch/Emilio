# NEW MASTERS

In August 1071, near the town of Manzikert, far to the east, the Byzantine emperor Romanos the Fourth met the army of the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan. The Byzantine army was defeated and the emperor himself was captured. In the years of confusion that followed, Turkish groups spread across the Anatolian plateau, and Cappadocia passed out of Byzantine control.

A new state grew up on the plateau, the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, with its capital at Konya. Its sultans built mosques, schools and, along the trade roads, great fortified inns for caravans. One of the finest, the Sultanhanı near Aksaray, was built in 1229 on the road that runs west of Cappadocia.
> WS | slow push | photo | neutral | | The Seljuk caravanserai of Sultanhanı near Aksaray {LT: Sultanhanı caravanserai | near Aksaray, 1229} [q: Sultanhani caravanserai Aksaray]

Caravanserais like this were built along the main trade roads of the sultanate, roughly a day's travel apart, so that merchants, their animals and their goods could shelter behind high walls for the night. Inside there was a courtyard, rooms, stables and often a small mosque. The roads that the Assyrian merchants of Kanesh had used three thousand years earlier were busy again, and the sultans protected them because trade paid for their state.

The Christians of Cappadocia did not disappear. Under the Seljuks and later under the Ottoman sultans, Christian villages survived across the region, side by side with Muslim ones. Churches went on being cut and painted, more modestly than before. In the valleys there are Greek inscriptions from the Seljuk period and later.

Under the Ottomans, who took over Cappadocia in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the region became a quiet province of the empire, far from the great cities. Villages were taxed, soldiers recruited, and roads maintained, but there were also long periods of insecurity, of bandits and local revolts. The underground rooms beneath the villages, still known to the people who lived above them, kept their old purpose as stores and, when needed, as hiding places.

Many of these Christians gradually came to speak Turkish as their everyday language while keeping their Orthodox faith. They are known as the Karamanlides. They wrote Turkish in the Greek alphabet, and from the eighteenth century onwards printed whole books this way, Bibles, prayer books and newspapers, in a script that today almost no one can read.

In the nineteenth century, some of these villages grew rich. Men went to work in Constantinople and sent money home, and their families built fine stone mansions. At Sinasos, today Mustafapaşa, a few kilometres from Göreme, these houses still stand, with carved facades and painted ceilings.

And in many of these villages, under the houses, the old underground rooms were still there.
