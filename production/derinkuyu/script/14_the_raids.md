# REFUGE FROM THE RAIDS

We are on firmer ground with the period when the city was used most. From the seventh century AD, Cappadocia became a frontier. To the south-east lay the new Islamic caliphate, to the west the Christian Byzantine Empire, and for roughly three centuries armies and raiding parties crossed this plateau again and again.

The raiders came through the passes of the Taurus Mountains, often in summer, seizing animals, crops and captives. A farming village on an open plain could not hold out against them. But a village that could vanish underground, with its animals, its grain and its water, could simply wait until the raiders moved on.

That is why most historians think the underground cities were greatly enlarged in the middle Byzantine period. The Byzantine army itself recommended a similar answer. When a large raiding army came, its commanders were told to avoid a pitched battle and to move the people of the countryside into strongholds, leaving the raiders little to take.

How close did the danger come? In the summer of 806, the caliph Harun al-Rashid, famous today from the tales of the Thousand and One Nights, led one of the largest Abbasid invasions of Byzantine Anatolia. His army came up through the Cilician Gates and reached Tyana, a city about sixty kilometres south of Derinkuyu, where he ordered a mosque to be built.

While the caliph besieged the fortress of Heraclea, part of his army was sent to plunder central Cappadocia. Arab historians put that detachment at tens of thousands of men. No source tells us what the villagers of Cappadocia did that summer. But this is exactly the kind of raid the underground cities were made for.

The frontier left its mark on Byzantine literature too. The epic of Digenis Akritis, the most famous poem of the Byzantine world, tells of a hero born of an Arab emir and a Byzantine noblewoman, who guards the borderlands of the east against raiders and bandits. Its name means the two-blooded border lord. It is a story, not a history, but it is set in exactly this world of raids, ambushes and frontier castles.

Thirty-two years later, in 838, another caliph, al-Mu'tasim, struck even harder. One of his armies came up through the Cilician Gates and crossed Cappadocia to take Ancyra, modern Ankara. Then the combined Abbasid forces besieged Amorium, the home city of the reigning Byzantine dynasty. After about two weeks the city was taken and sacked, and thousands of its people were killed or carried off.

For the people of a Cappadocian village, campaigns like these were not distant news. The armies marched through their fields. When the frontier finally moved east in the tenth century, the danger did not end for good. Later centuries would bring new invaders and new wars, and each time the cities underground could be opened and filled again.
