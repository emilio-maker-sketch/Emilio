# AIR AND WATER

A city eighty-five metres underground has two problems that matter more than anything else. People need to breathe, and they need to drink. Derinkuyu solved both with the same idea: vertical shafts cut straight down through the rock.

Some shafts run from the surface down through the levels, bringing fresh air to every floor. Air moves through them because the underground stays at much the same cool temperature all year while the air outside changes, so the difference pushes air in and out. The system still works: even on the lowest levels open to visitors, the air is fresh enough to breathe without difficulty.

Other shafts were wells. They were dug down to the groundwater, and the deepest is reported to reach about fifty-five metres. A well that could be reached from inside the city meant that people sheltering below did not have to go up for water, the most dangerous moment of any siege.

Two Turkish engineers, Ömer Aydan and Reşat Ulusay, have studied Derinkuyu's rock and structure for years. Their work shows why the city has lasted. The tuff is weak compared with granite or limestone, but the builders kept rooms small, left thick pillars and walls between them, and stacked the levels so that each one stood on solid rock.

None of this needed advanced technology. It needed patience, experience and a very good understanding of this one kind of rock. The people who dug Derinkuyu learned what tuff would and would not do, probably over many generations, and passed that knowledge on.
