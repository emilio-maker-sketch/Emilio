# WHAT WE ACTUALLY KNOW

Now we can return to the claims we started with. Eighteen levels? No published survey supports it. At least eight levels are known, and the official figure for the open part of the city is eight. More may lie below, but eighteen is a number that has been repeated, not measured.

Twenty thousand people? That figure appears in newspapers and travel guides, but none of them names the researcher who calculated it. The city was large enough to shelter many families with their animals. How many, no one has shown. The honest statement is that estimates run as high as twenty thousand.

Xenophon? He described underground houses in Armenia, not Cappadocia. And the claims that giants, a lost civilisation or visitors from elsewhere carved it have no evidence behind them at all. Everything about Derinkuyu, from its tool marks to its church, points to ordinary people, working with simple tools, over a very long time.

And the tunnel to Kaymaklı? Reported, but not mapped or published from end to end. The church and the rolling doors? Real, and you can see them. The fifty-five-metre well? Reported in recent research. The Phrygians? Possible, not proven. The Byzantine refuges from the Arab raids? The best supported part of the whole story. Sorting the claims like this does not make Derinkuyu smaller. It shows which parts of the story stand on solid rock.

Which leaves the real answers to our three questions. How was it built? By hand, in a soft rock that its builders understood perfectly, one room at a time. Who built it? Many generations of Cappadocians, whose names are lost, ending with the Christian villagers of Malakopi. And why? Because for centuries this plateau was a frontier, and the safest place to be was underground.

There is one more thing worth saying. The real story is better than the myths. A city dug by giants or aliens would be a curiosity. A city dug by farmers, with iron picks, by lamplight, over centuries, to keep their children and their animals alive while armies marched over their heads, is something else: one of the great achievements of ordinary people anywhere in the ancient and medieval world.

Walk back up the stairs and out into the daylight, and the town looks ordinary again: houses, a mosque, a main street. Under your feet, eighty-five metres of rock are still hollow, a reminder that for a very long time the people of Cappadocia survived by hiding. And they hid so well that their hiding place was almost forgotten.
