# CHAPTER ONE: THE LAND OF SOFT STONE

To understand Derinkuyu you have to start with the ground it was cut from. Cappadocia lies on the high plateau of central Anatolia. Millions of years ago, volcanoes such as Erciyes and Hasan buried this landscape under thick layers of ash. Pressed and settled over time, the ash became a pale, soft rock called tuff.

The volcanoes were not only ancient history. Mount Hasan, west of Derinkuyu, erupted within the time of human memory. Geologists who dated its youngest rocks found an eruption about nine thousand years ago, and some archaeologists think a wall painting at Çatalhöyük, a Neolithic town on the Konya plain, shows a twin-peaked volcano erupting above a settlement. Whether the painting really shows Hasan is still debated. But the people of this region have lived beside volcanoes for as long as there have been people here.

Wind and water carved the tuff into the strange cones and pillars that make Cappadocia famous today, the so-called fairy chimneys. Where a harder cap of rock protected the softer tuff below, a tall pillar was left standing as the land around it wore away.
> WS | slow push | photo | neutral | | Fairy chimney with a dark cap stone [q: fairy chimney cap rock Cappadocia]

The same softness made it easy to dig. With iron tools a person can cut tuff almost like hard clay, yet a room carved in it will stand for centuries. So for thousands of years the people of Cappadocia did not only build upward. They dug in. They cut houses, pigeon houses, storerooms and churches into the cliffs and the cones.

Carving had another advantage. A carved room needs no timber for its roof, and timber was scarce on this treeless plateau. It needs no mortar and no bricks. And it stays cool in summer and holds its warmth in winter. For poor farmers, a room cut into the rock was cheaper and more comfortable than one built on top of it.

Underground settlements were the next step down. Dozens of them have been recorded across the region, and new ones are still being found. Most are small. A few are enormous. The two largest open to visitors lie only a few kilometres apart, at Kaymaklı and here at Derinkuyu.

Today these valleys are protected together with the rock churches of Göreme. UNESCO listed them as a World Heritage Site in 1985, under the name Göreme National Park and the Rock Sites of Cappadocia. Derinkuyu is one of the sites named in that listing.
