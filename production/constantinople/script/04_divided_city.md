# CHAPTER FOUR: A DIVIDED CITY

Constantine knew he could not defend the city alone. The only real hope was help from the Catholic West: from Venice, Genoa, the pope, the kings of Hungary and Aragon. But that help came with a price. For centuries, the Orthodox and Catholic churches had been divided. The West had made it clear that it expected the Greeks to accept union with Rome, on Rome's terms.

On the twelfth of December 1452, in Hagia Sophia, the union of the churches was formally proclaimed. The ceremony was led by Cardinal Isidore, a Greek-born churchman who had come from Rome with a small force of about two hundred soldiers. The pope's name was read out in the liturgy. To many of the Orthodox faithful, this was a betrayal. Crowds gathered in protest, and many people refused to set foot in the great church afterwards.

One phrase from those months has become famous. The historian Doukas attributes it to Loukas Notaras, the grand duke, the most senior official in the empire after the emperor. Better, he is supposed to have said, to see the turban of the Turks ruling in the city than the Latin mitre. Whether Notaras ever really said it, we cannot be sure. But it captures the bitterness that divided the city on the eve of its greatest danger.

Help did come, though far less than hoped. The most important arrival was a Genoese soldier named Giovanni Giustiniani Longo, an experienced specialist in the defence of walled towns. In late January 1453, he sailed in with two ships and around seven hundred well-armed men, many recruited on his own initiative. Constantine gave him command of the defence of the land walls.

Others were there too. There were Venetian merchants and sailors who decided to stay and fight, under their governor in the city, Girolamo Minotto. There were Catalans, Cretans, a few men from other parts of Europe, and even the Ottoman prince Orhan with his own followers, who had every reason to fight, since he could expect no mercy from Mehmed. Not everyone stayed, though. One night in late February, several ships slipped out of the harbour and sailed away, taking hundreds of people with them.

The emperor ordered a careful count of every man able to fight. According to Sphrantzes, who carried out the count himself, the result was shocking: four thousand, seven hundred and seventy-three Greeks, and only about two hundred foreigners. Constantine told him to keep the number secret. Historians think the full force, including all the Italian contingents, was perhaps seven or eight thousand men. They had to defend around twenty kilometres of walls.

Through the winter and early spring, the defenders prepared. The walls were repaired and the moat cleared. Weapons were collected and handed out. And at the start of April, a huge iron chain on wooden floats was stretched across the entrance of the Golden Horn, from the city to the walls of Galata on the other side, to keep the Ottoman fleet out of the harbour.
