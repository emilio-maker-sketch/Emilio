# CHAPTER NINE: THE WAR UNDERGROUND

By May, the siege had settled into a grinding routine. By day, the guns fired. By night, the defenders repaired the breaches. Food was running short, and men on the walls had to leave their posts to find bread for their families. Tensions between Greeks, Venetians and Genoese grew worse, with each side accusing the others of not doing their share.

The Ottomans kept testing the defences. In early May, they launched a heavy night attack near the Gate of Saint Romanus. A few days later, they struck at the walls near the Palace of Blachernae, at the northern end of the land walls. Both attacks were beaten back. Around this time, some of the emperor's advisers are said to have urged him to leave the city, to rally help from outside while there was still time. He refused. His place, he said, was with his people.

Mehmed now tried a different approach. Among his troops were experienced silver miners from Serbia, from the mines of Novo Brdo. In mid-May, they began to dig tunnels toward the walls, aiming to get underneath them and collapse them, or to break into the city from below.

The defenders had their own expert. He was an engineer known as Johannes Grant, whom Barbaro calls a German, although some historians have suggested he was Scottish. Grant organised the countermining. His men listened, in cellars and at the foot of the walls, for the faint sound of picks in the earth. Then they dug their own tunnels to meet the enemy underground.

What followed was a terrifying war in the dark. When the tunnels met, the two sides fought hand to hand underground, by lamplight, in spaces barely wide enough to stand. The defenders flooded tunnels, filled them with smoke, or burned the wooden supports to bring the roof down on the miners. Over the following days, they found and destroyed tunnel after tunnel.

There was another danger above ground. One morning, the defenders woke to find that a huge wooden siege tower had been built during the night in front of the walls, covered with hides to protect it from fire. Under its cover, the attackers were filling in the moat. That night, the defenders crept out and destroyed it with barrels of gunpowder.

Meanwhile, the city waited for help that never seemed to come. In early May, a small ship had slipped out past the Ottoman fleet, its crew disguised, to search the Aegean for the relief fleet promised by Venice. On the twenty-third of May, it came back. The sailors had found nothing. According to Barbaro, they had discussed whether to sail on to safety, and decided instead that it was their duty to return and tell the emperor, whatever the cost.

Constantine is said to have wept. There would be no fleet. There would be no crusade. Whatever happened now, the city would face it alone.
