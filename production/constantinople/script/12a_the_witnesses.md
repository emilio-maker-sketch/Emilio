# CHAPTER FOURTEEN: THE WITNESSES

How do we know any of this? The fall of Constantinople is one of the best-documented events of the Middle Ages, but it is not as simple as it looks. Almost everything we know comes from a small group of writers, and each of them saw the siege from a different place, at a different time, and for different reasons.

Nicolò Barbaro was a Venetian ship's doctor who kept a diary, day by day, through the whole siege. He is precise about dates and events in the harbour. But he is fiercely loyal to Venice, quick to praise Venetians and to blame the Genoese for almost everything that went wrong. Leonard of Chios, a Catholic archbishop who was also in the city, wrote a long letter to the pope soon after its fall. He was bitter about the Greeks, many of whom had rejected the union of the churches, and he blamed them for their own disaster.

On the Greek side, there is Sphrantzes, the emperor's friend, whose short chronicle is personal and grief-stricken. There is Doukas, a Greek in the service of the Genoese, who wrote a vivid history a few years later and recorded many of the most dramatic stories, including the Kerkoporta. There is Laonikos Chalkokondyles, an Athenian historian who wrote about the rise of the Ottomans. And there is Kritoboulos of Imbros, who wrote his history for Mehmed himself, and presents the sultan as a heroic new Alexander.

From the Ottoman side, the most important early source is Tursun Beg, an official who was present at the siege as a young man and wrote about it decades later in praise of the sultan. There is also a famous Slavonic account attributed to a man named Nestor-Iskander, who claims to have been a Christian serving in the Ottoman army. How much of it is genuine eyewitness testimony is still debated.

That is why historians argue about the numbers, the dates and the details. When the sources agree, we can be fairly confident. When a dramatic story appears in only one of them, we should be careful. And when an account was written long after the event, or for a patron with a reason to shape the story, we have to ask why it was told the way it was. The story in this film follows the sources as closely as possible, and tells you when they disagree.
