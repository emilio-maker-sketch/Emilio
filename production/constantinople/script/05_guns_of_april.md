# CHAPTER FIVE: THE GUNS OF APRIL

Easter fell on the first of April in 1453. The next day, the first Ottoman troops appeared outside the walls. Over the following days, the main army arrived and spread out along the entire length of the land walls, from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn. On the fifth or sixth of April, Mehmed himself pitched his great tent opposite the Gate of Saint Romanus, facing the weakest section of the defences.

How big was the army? Christian eyewitnesses gave enormous figures, some of them in the hundreds of thousands. Those numbers are almost certainly too high. Modern historians generally estimate that the Ottoman force was somewhere between fifty and eighty thousand fighting men, with many more camp followers, labourers and volunteers. Even so, the defenders were outnumbered by perhaps ten to one.

At the heart of the army were the janissaries, the sultan's elite infantry. They were originally recruited as boys from Christian families in the Balkans, converted to Islam, and trained as professional soldiers loyal only to the sultan. There were also the sipahis, cavalrymen who held land in return for military service, irregular troops and volunteers, Christian vassals from the Balkans, and specialists of every kind: engineers, miners, gunners and smiths.

The guns were dragged into place and set up in batteries along the walls, protected by earthworks and wooden screens. The great bombard was positioned near the sultan's tent, aimed at the Gate of Saint Romanus. On the sixth of April, the bombardment began. Within days, it was continuous, day after day, for weeks.

The giant gun was slow. Loading, aiming and cooling it took hours, and it could fire only a handful of times a day. But when its shots struck, whole sections of masonry collapsed. And it was only one of many. Dozens of smaller guns kept up a steady fire, and the walls, built to resist the weapons of the ancient world, began to crumble.

Every night, the defenders went out to repair the damage. Under Giustiniani's direction, they filled the breaches with whatever they could find: earth, stones, timber, brushwood, barrels packed with soil. These improvised barricades turned out to absorb the impact of the cannonballs better than the old stone walls. By morning, a gap smashed open the day before would be blocked again.
