# CHAPTER TWELVE: THE FALL

The attack began with a terrifying noise. Drums, trumpets, cymbals and the battle cries of tens of thousands of men rose all at once along the walls. In the city, the church bells began to ring in alarm. People who were not fighting ran to the churches to pray. The assault came at many points at the same time, but Mehmed's real target was the Lycus valley.

The first wave was made up largely of irregular troops and volunteers. They were poorly armed but numerous, and they threw themselves at the barricades with ladders for about two hours. The defenders cut them down with crossbows, handguns, arrows and stones. When the survivors fell back, the defenders were exhausted, but the line had held.

The second wave came immediately afterwards: disciplined regular troops from Anatolia. They pressed hard against the barricades, and at one point, after a cannonball smashed through part of the stockade, a few hundred of them broke in. They were surrounded and driven out. Again, the line held.

By now, the sky was beginning to lighten. The defenders had been fighting for hours. Then Mehmed sent in his last and best troops: the janissaries. They advanced in good order, to the sound of military music, fresh and disciplined. The sultan himself is said to have led them as far as the edge of the moat.

For about an hour, the fighting at the barricade was as fierce as anything in the whole siege. Then, at the most critical moment, Giustiniani was seriously wounded. Accounts differ about how, and where he was hit. What is clear is that he was carried away from the wall, through a gate in the inner wall, and down to one of the Genoese ships in the harbour.

For his men, the effect was catastrophic. Seeing their commander carried off, many of the Genoese thought the battle was lost, and they began to follow him through the gate. The defence of the stockade began to break apart. At almost the same moment, according to Doukas, a group of Ottoman soldiers found a small gate further north, called the Kerkoporta, which had been left unlocked, entered the space between the walls, and raised their banners on a tower. Some historians accept this story. Others doubt it, because it appears in only one source. But either way, the cry soon spread along the walls: the city is taken.

The janissaries poured over the barricades. The defenders on the outer walls were trapped between the walls, and many were killed there. Elsewhere along the walls, as news spread, defenders abandoned their positions to try to reach their families. Within a short time, the Ottoman army was inside the city.

And the emperor? The truth is that nobody knows exactly how Constantine died. The most widely repeated account says that when he saw that the battle was lost, he threw away his imperial insignia and charged into the enemy with a few companions, and was never seen again. Some writers said his body was identified later by the imperial eagles on his boots. Others told different stories. His body was never certainly found, and no tomb exists.

It did not take long for legends to grow. In Greek folklore, the emperor never died at all. An angel turned him to marble and hid him in a cave beneath the city, where he sleeps, waiting for the day he will wake and take Constantinople back. They called him the Marble Emperor.
