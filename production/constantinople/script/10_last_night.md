# CHAPTER ELEVEN: THE LAST NIGHT

The twenty-eighth of May was a Monday. In the Ottoman camp, the soldiers fasted and prayed. Mehmed rode along the lines, inspecting the troops and speaking to the commanders. Ladders, shields and siege equipment were brought forward. The guns were moved closer. In the city, the defenders knew exactly what this silence meant.

Inside the walls, the bells rang, and icons and relics were carried along the walls in procession. People of every class took part, Greeks and Italians, Orthodox and Catholic, singing hymns and calling out the Kyrie eleison, Lord have mercy. According to the accounts, Constantine gathered his commanders and the leaders of the Italians and spoke to them.

The longest version of his speech that survives comes from a chronicle written more than a century later, which drew on Sphrantzes's work but greatly expanded it, and historians treat its wording with suspicion. But the shorter contemporary accounts agree on its spirit. He asked his men to fight for their faith, their country, their families and their emperor. He thanked the Italians for staying. And he told them that, whatever happened, he would stand with them.

Then, as night fell, came the service in Hagia Sophia. For the first time in months, Orthodox and Catholics prayed together under the great dome. The emperor took communion, and, we are told, asked forgiveness of everyone present for any wrong he had done them. Then he rode to the Palace of Blachernae, said farewell to his household, and went out to the walls.

The gates between the inner and outer walls were locked behind the defenders. There would be no retreat. Most of the men had now taken their places on the outer wall and the stockades, where they would fight. Giustiniani and his Genoese, with many of the best Greek troops, held the most dangerous section, the Lycus valley, where the walls had been smashed and replaced with barricades of earth and timber.
