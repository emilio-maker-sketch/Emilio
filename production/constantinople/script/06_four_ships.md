# CHAPTER SEVEN: FOUR SHIPS

Mehmed also had a fleet. Eyewitnesses counted well over a hundred vessels, most of them galleys and smaller oared ships, under the command of an admiral named Baltaoğlu Süleyman. The fleet's task was to blockade the city from the sea and break into the Golden Horn. But the chain held, and behind it, the larger Christian sailing ships, with their high sides, were very difficult to attack.

On the twentieth of April, the defenders saw sails on the horizon. Four ships were approaching from the south through the Sea of Marmara: three Genoese ships, sent with supplies and weapons with the support of the pope, and a large imperial transport carrying grain. Mehmed immediately ordered his fleet to stop them, whatever the cost.

What followed was watched by thousands of people from the city walls and from the shore. The Ottoman galleys surrounded the four ships and tried to board them. The sailors on the tall ships fought back from above, hurling stones, javelins and arrows down onto the galleys, and pouring fire on any boat that came alongside. For a time, they kept moving forward.

Then, close to the city, the wind dropped. The sails went slack, and the ships began to drift toward the shore where the sultan was watching. The Ottoman galleys closed in. The captains of the four ships lashed their vessels together, forming a single floating fortress, and fought on.

Mehmed was so desperate to win that, according to several accounts, he rode his horse into the sea, shouting orders and abuse at his commanders, until his robes were soaked. On the walls, the people of Constantinople prayed.

Late in the afternoon, the wind returned. The sails filled, and the four ships broke through the circle of galleys. As darkness fell, the chain was lowered, and they sailed safely into the Golden Horn. Not one of them had been lost.

For the defenders, it was a wonderful moment. For Mehmed, it was a humiliation in front of his entire army. Admiral Baltaoğlu, who had been wounded in the eye during the battle, was stripped of his command. Some accounts say he was beaten in front of the sultan. Mehmed had learned a hard lesson. He could not win at sea in the ordinary way. He would need to do something no one expected.
