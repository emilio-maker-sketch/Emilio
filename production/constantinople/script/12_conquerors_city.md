# CHAPTER THIRTEEN: THE CONQUEROR'S CITY

What followed was the sack. Soldiers spread through the city, looting houses, churches and monasteries. Many people were killed in the first hours, especially near the walls. But most of the population was not massacred. They were captured. Men, women and children were roped together and taken away to be sold as slaves or held for ransom. Kritoboulos, a Greek who later served Mehmed, puts the number of captives at around fifty thousand. Whatever the exact figure, almost the entire population of the city lost its freedom that day.

Thousands had taken refuge in Hagia Sophia, believing, according to one tradition, that an angel would come down to save the city when the enemy reached the great church. When the soldiers broke down the doors, the people inside were taken captive.

In the harbour, sailors cut through the chain and some of the Italian ships escaped, crowded with refugees. Barbaro was on one of them, and so he lived to write his diary. Cardinal Isidore escaped disguised as a slave. Prince Orhan was killed trying to escape. Sphrantzes was captured, but was later ransomed, and lived for many years.

In the afternoon, Mehmed entered the city. He rode to Hagia Sophia. Accounts say that he dismounted, knelt, and placed a handful of earth on his turban as a sign of humility. When he found a soldier breaking up the marble floor, he struck him and ordered that the building should not be damaged. Then he ordered that the church be turned into a mosque. On the following Friday, the first of June, prayers were held there for the first time.

There is a famous story that Mehmed, walking through the ruins of the old imperial palace, recited a few lines of Persian poetry about the spider weaving curtains in the palace of the Caesars, and the owl calling from the towers. The story is told by later Ottoman writers, and we cannot be sure it happened. But it may tell us how he saw what he had done. He was not destroying an empire. He was inheriting one.

In the days that followed, the sultan stopped the sack, earlier than the three days that custom allowed, according to some accounts. Loukas Notaras, who had survived, was at first treated with respect, but within days he was executed along with his sons. The Venetian governor Minotto was also executed. And then Mehmed set out to make the city great again.

He brought in settlers from across his empire, Muslims, Christians and Jews alike, and in some cases forced whole communities to move there. He appointed a new Orthodox patriarch, the scholar Gennadios Scholarios, who had been one of the fiercest opponents of the union with Rome, and gave him authority over the empire's Orthodox Christians. He built a new palace, mosques, markets and baths. And he took a new title. Among his many titles, he called himself Kayser-i Rum, the Caesar of Rome.
