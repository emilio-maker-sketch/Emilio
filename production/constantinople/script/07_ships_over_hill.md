# CHAPTER EIGHT: SHIPS OVER THE HILL

Across the Golden Horn from Constantinople stood Galata, also called Pera, a walled town that belonged to the Republic of Genoa. Officially, it was neutral. Its people traded with both sides, and many of them quietly helped the city. Mehmed left Galata alone. But behind its walls rose a range of steep hills, and the sultan had noticed something about them.

In the days after the naval defeat, his engineers built a road from the shore of the Bosphorus, up over the hills behind Galata, and down to the waters of the Golden Horn on the far side. Wooden rails were laid along it and greased with fat. Wheeled cradles were built. And on the twenty-second of April, the Ottomans began to drag their ships overland.

Teams of oxen and men hauled the galleys out of the water, onto the cradles and up the slope. Accounts describe the ships moving with their sails raised, with oarsmen at their benches pulling at the air as if they were rowing across the land, while drums and music played. Within a single day, a large part of the Ottoman fleet had crossed the hills. Most accounts put the number at around seventy ships.

The defenders woke to find the enemy's ships floating inside their own harbour, behind the chain. The sea walls along the Golden Horn, which had been lightly guarded because they were thought to be safe, were now exposed. Men had to be taken from the land walls to defend them.

The Venetians decided to strike back. On the night of the twenty-eighth of April, a small group of ships under a captain named Giacomo Coco tried to slip across the harbour and burn the Ottoman fleet. But the Ottomans were waiting. Their guns opened fire as soon as the ships were in range, and Coco's ship was hit and sank within moments. The attack collapsed. The Venetians later accused the Genoese of Pera of warning the sultan. The Genoese denied it. We do not know the truth.

What followed was one of the darkest episodes of the siege. Sailors who had swum ashore were captured and executed by the Ottomans in full view of the walls. In response, the defenders executed Ottoman prisoners on the battlements, where the besieging army could see. Barbaro, the Venetian ship's doctor whose diary is one of our best sources, recorded both.

Soon afterwards, Ottoman engineers built a floating bridge of barrels and planks across the upper part of the Golden Horn, so that troops and guns could move easily between the camp and the harbour walls. The noose was tightening.
