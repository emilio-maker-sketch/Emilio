# CHAPTER TEN: SIGNS AND OMENS

The people of Constantinople lived surrounded by prophecies. Old texts and popular legends predicted the end of the city, and the end of the world, in many different ways. In the last week of May, as morale collapsed, people began to see signs everywhere. Several of them were written down by the chroniclers who lived through the siege.

On the night of the twenty-second of May, there was a partial eclipse of the moon. This is not legend. Astronomers can calculate it precisely. For people who already believed that the end was near, the sight of the moon darkening over the city was terrifying.

Soon afterwards, the clergy carried the city's most sacred icon, an image of the Virgin Mary believed to protect Constantinople, through the streets in a great procession. According to the accounts, the icon slipped from the hands of those carrying it and fell to the ground. It was so heavy that it took a long time to lift it again. Then a violent storm broke over the city, with thunder and hail and rain so heavy that the streets flooded, and the procession had to be abandoned.

The next day, a thick fog covered the city, which was very unusual for late May. And that night, according to several writers, a strange light was seen around the dome of Hagia Sophia, moving slowly upwards and disappearing into the sky. Some took it to mean that the divine presence was leaving the city. We cannot know exactly what they saw. But we do know what they believed it meant.

Outside the walls, Mehmed faced his own problems. The siege had lasted seven weeks. His soldiers were exhausted, and rumours spread that a Hungarian army or a Venetian fleet was on its way. At a council of war, the old grand vizier, Halil Pasha, argued that the siege should be lifted before disaster struck. Others, led by the commander Zaganos Pasha, insisted on a final assault.

Mehmed chose to attack. According to some accounts, he had already sent a final offer to Constantine: if the city surrendered, the emperor could leave freely for the Peloponnese, and the people would be spared. Constantine is said to have replied that it was not in his power, or in the power of anyone in the city, to give it away. They would all die rather than surrender it.

The date was set for the twenty-ninth of May. Heralds rode through the camp announcing that, by custom, the city would be given to the soldiers to plunder for three days if it was taken by storm. On the night of the twenty-seventh, the defenders on the walls saw the Ottoman camp light up with fires and torches, and heard the sound of drums and music. The next day, everything fell silent.
