# CHAPTER TWO: THE EMPEROR AND THE SULTAN

In January 1449, a man of forty-three was proclaimed emperor at Mystras, in southern Greece. His name was Constantine Palaiologos. He had spent much of his life as a governor and soldier in the Peloponnese, fighting to hold together what was left of the empire there. He had been married twice, and both wives had died young. He had no children. When he arrived in Constantinople that spring, he inherited a treasury that was nearly empty and an empire that was barely an empire at all.

We know surprisingly little about what Constantine was like. No reliable portrait of him survives. Most of what we know comes from a handful of chroniclers, some of whom knew him well. His closest friend, the courtier George Sphrantzes, later wrote a short chronicle of his own life, and it is one of our most valuable sources. What comes through in all of them is a man more experienced in war than in politics, stubborn, brave, and keenly aware of how weak his position was.

Two years later, in February 1451, the Ottoman sultan Murad the Second died at Edirne. Murad had attacked Constantinople once before, in 1422, and failed. In his later years, he had preferred to leave the city alone. His heir was his son Mehmed, who was not yet nineteen.

Mehmed had already been sultan once. In 1444, when he was twelve, his father stepped down in his favour, but the experiment failed. A crusader army invaded the Balkans, and Murad had to come back to lead the army, crushing the crusaders at Varna. Two years later, after a revolt by the janissaries, Murad took the throne back for good, and Mehmed was sent away to the provinces. That humiliation, historians often suggest, may help explain what came next.

The young sultan was clever, well educated and intensely ambitious. Contemporaries said he read history and spoke several languages, although exactly how many is debated. He was also secretive and could be ruthless. Almost as soon as he took power, his infant half-brother was killed, a grim practice that would become standard in Ottoman succession. Mehmed made peace with his neighbours, confirmed old treaties, and gave every sign of wanting quiet on his frontiers.

In Constantinople, some took this as a sign that the new sultan was weak. That was a serious mistake. In 1451, the emperor's envoys complained about payments for an Ottoman prince, Orhan, who lived as a kind of hostage in the city, and hinted that he might be released as a rival claimant to the throne. According to the Greek historian Doukas, the grand vizier Halil Pasha, who was the strongest voice for peace at the Ottoman court, was furious. He told them, in Doukas's account, that they were fools, that the new sultan was nothing like his gentle father, and that if they kept pushing, they would lose even what little they had.

For Mehmed, Constantinople was more than a strategic prize. For centuries, Muslim armies had dreamed of taking it. Arab forces had besieged the city in the seventh and early eighth centuries and failed. A saying attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, and much repeated at the Ottoman court, promised that Constantinople would one day be conquered, and praised the commander and the army that would do it. Among those said to have died beneath its walls during the early Arab sieges was Abu Ayyub al-Ansari, a companion of the Prophet. The young sultan wanted to be the commander that saying described.
