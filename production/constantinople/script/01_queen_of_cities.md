# CHAPTER ONE: THE QUEEN OF CITIES

To understand why so many people wanted this city, you have to see where it stands. Constantinople sat on a triangle of land at the edge of Europe. To the south lay the Sea of Marmara. To the north, a deep, sheltered harbour called the Golden Horn. And to the east, the Bosphorus, the narrow strait that separates Europe from Asia and connects the Mediterranean world to the Black Sea. Whoever held this point controlled the crossing between two continents and the sea route between two seas.

The Roman emperor Constantine the Great refounded the old Greek town of Byzantion here and dedicated it as his new capital in the year 330. Within two centuries, it had become the largest and richest city in Europe. When the western half of the Roman Empire collapsed in the fifth century, the eastern half did not. It carried on, ruled from Constantinople, for another thousand years.

We call that state the Byzantine Empire, but that is a name invented by later historians. The people who lived in it never used it. They called themselves Romans, Romaioi, and their state was simply the Roman Empire. They spoke Greek, they were Orthodox Christians, and they saw themselves as the direct continuation of the empire of Augustus and Constantine. In a real sense, they were.

What protected this city, above all, were its walls. In the early fifth century, under the emperor Theodosius the Second, a new line of land walls was built across the peninsula, from the Sea of Marmara to the Golden Horn, a distance of nearly six kilometres. It was not one wall but a system. First came a broad moat. Behind it, a lower outer wall. And behind that, the great inner wall, around twelve metres high, studded with nearly a hundred towers.

For a thousand years, these defences held. Huns, Avars, Persians, Arabs, Bulgars and Rus all came, and all went away again. Only once had the city been taken by storm. In 1204, the knights of the Fourth Crusade, who had set out to fight in the Holy Land, turned on Constantinople instead, broke in from the harbour side and sacked it. The empire was recovered in 1261, but it never recovered from the blow.

By the fifteenth century, the empire had shrunk to almost nothing. A new power had risen in Anatolia and the Balkans: the Ottoman Turks. Step by step, they absorbed the lands around the city, until the Roman Empire was little more than Constantinople itself, a few islands, and some territory in southern Greece. The capital was now an island in an Ottoman sea.

Inside the walls, the change was obvious. In its prime, the city may have had several hundred thousand inhabitants. By 1453, most estimates put it at around forty or fifty thousand. Travellers described whole districts of ruins, fields and orchards inside the walls, and villages clustered around old churches. The great palaces were crumbling. A century earlier, an empress had pawned the crown jewels to Venice, and no one had ever been able to buy them back. And yet the city still held something no army had ever taken from it: the idea that it was the centre of the world.
