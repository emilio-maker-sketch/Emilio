# CHAPTER FIFTEEN: THE LAST DAY OF ROME

The news reached Venice, Rome and the courts of Europe within weeks. The shock was enormous. The pope called for a crusade to take the city back, but no crusade ever came. Within a few years, the last Byzantine territories also fell: the Peloponnese in 1460, and the Empire of Trebizond, a separate Greek state on the Black Sea coast, in 1461.

You may have heard that the fall of Constantinople caused the Renaissance, as Greek scholars fled to Italy, carrying ancient manuscripts with them. That is only partly true. Greek scholars had been teaching in Italy for more than half a century before 1453, and the Renaissance was already well under way. The refugees added to a movement that already existed. They did not start it.

You may also have heard that it closed the trade routes to Asia and forced Europeans to sail around the world, leading to Columbus. That idea is very popular, and historians have largely rejected it. Trade with the East continued through the Ottoman Empire, and the Portuguese were exploring the African coast long before 1453.

So why does 1453 matter? Because on that day, a state that could trace its history back, without a break, to the Rome of Augustus came to an end. Many historians have used it as one of the dates that mark the end of the Middle Ages. It was also a clear sign of a new age of warfare: the walls that had defended the city for a thousand years had been broken, in large part, by gunpowder. And it marked the rise of a new great power. For almost five centuries, the city would be the capital of the Ottoman Empire.

It was never really a city of endings. The Ottomans called it Kostantiniyye, the city of Constantine, for centuries. Over time, another name came into common use, one that probably comes from a Greek phrase meaning, simply, to the city. In 1930, it became the official name. Istanbul.

And if you go there today, you can still walk along the land walls of Theodosius. Large stretches of them still stand, battered, patched, and half covered with grass and fig trees. In the valley of the Lycus, near the place where the barricades stood, the stones are still broken.

As far as we know, Constantine Palaiologos and Mehmed the Conqueror never met face to face. For fifty-three days, they faced each other across a few hundred metres of ground. One lost everything. The other became one of the most famous rulers in history. And between them, on a spring morning in 1453, the Roman Empire, after almost fifteen hundred years, came to an end.
