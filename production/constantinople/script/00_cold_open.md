# COLD OPEN

On the evening of the twenty-eighth of May, 1453, the bells of Constantinople rang out. The people of the city, Greeks and Italians, Orthodox and Catholic, who for months had refused to pray together, came in their thousands to the great church of Hagia Sophia. For almost a thousand years, it had been the largest church in the Christian world. That night, it may have been the last place in the city where anyone felt safe.

Outside, beyond the land walls, the noise had stopped. For a day and a night, an army of tens of thousands had been fasting and resting in near silence. Its guns were loaded. Its ladders were ready. And somewhere among the tents, a young sultan, just twenty-one years old, was waiting for the hours to pass.

Inside the church, the emperor is said to have asked those around him for forgiveness. Then he rode back to the walls. His name was Constantine, and he stood at the end of a line of rulers that ran back, through nearly fifteen centuries, to Augustus. He called himself what all of them had been called: Emperor of the Romans.

By the next evening, the Roman Empire, the oldest state in Europe, would be gone. How could a city that had stood behind its walls for a thousand years fall in fifty-three days? Who was the young ruler who did it? And what really happened on the last day of Rome?
