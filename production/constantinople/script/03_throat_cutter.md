# CHAPTER THREE: THE THROAT-CUTTER

In the spring of 1452, Ottoman workers began to arrive at a quiet stretch of the European shore of the Bosphorus, a few kilometres north of Constantinople. This was the narrowest point of the strait, where Europe and Asia are only about seven hundred metres apart. On the Asian side, there was already a small Ottoman fortress, built by Mehmed's great-grandfather. Now Mehmed would build its partner.

The speed of the work astonished everyone who saw it. Thousands of labourers and masons cut stone, burned lime and raised walls, and the sultan's senior commanders were each made responsible for building one of the great towers. Stone was taken from wherever it could be found, including, the Greek chroniclers bitterly noted, the ruins of churches. In roughly four and a half months, by the end of August, the fortress was finished. Its three great towers still stand on the hillside today.

The Ottomans called it Boğazkesen. The name can mean the Cutter of the Strait, but also the Throat-Cutter. Its cannons now covered the Bosphorus, and every ship sailing to or from the Black Sea was ordered to stop and pay a toll. It was a direct threat to the city's grain supply and to the trade of the Italian merchants who lived there.

In November 1452, a Venetian captain named Antonio Rizzo tried to sail past without stopping. The fortress opened fire. A single stone ball struck his ship and sank it. Rizzo and his crew were taken to the sultan. The crew were executed, and Rizzo was impaled and left beside the road as a warning. The Venetians in Constantinople, who had hoped to stay neutral, now understood exactly what was coming.

Meanwhile, a man had arrived at the sultan's court with an extraordinary offer. His name was Orban, or Urban, and he was a cannon founder, probably from Hungary or Transylvania. According to Doukas, he had first gone to Constantinople and offered his services to the emperor, who could not afford to pay what he asked. So he went to the Ottomans instead.

Mehmed, the story goes, asked him a single question. Could he cast a gun large enough to break the walls of Constantinople? Orban said that he could. The sultan, who understood the value of new technology better than almost anyone of his age, gave him everything he needed.

Over the winter, at Edirne, Orban cast his greatest gun. The accounts that describe it are remarkable. Its barrel was said to be around eight metres long. It fired stone balls that, by the writers' own estimates, weighed several hundred kilograms. When it was tested, Doukas claims, the sound could be heard for miles, and the ball buried itself deep in the earth. To move it the two hundred kilometres to Constantinople took, he says, sixty oxen and hundreds of men, who smoothed the road and built bridges ahead of it.

Historians treat these numbers with some caution. Medieval writers often exaggerated. But there is no doubt that the Ottoman army was assembling the most powerful siege artillery the world had yet seen. The walls of Constantinople had been designed to stop men, rams and catapults. They had never faced anything like this.
