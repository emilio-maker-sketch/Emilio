# CHAPTER EIGHT: THE ISLAND OF COPPER

[v: cyprus coast]
Before we follow the storm to Egypt, we need to stop on an island that sat right in its path. Cyprus. In the letters of the Late Bronze Age, it was called Alashiya, and its kings wrote to Pharaoh as equals, calling him brother. The reason was simple. Copper.

[v: oxhide ingot cyprus]
The copper mines in the foothills of the Troodos Mountains were among the richest in the ancient world. In one of the Amarna letters, the king of Alashiya sends five hundred units of copper to Egypt, and apologizes that it is not more, explaining that the hand of the god Nergal, a god of plague and death, had killed all the men of his land, so that there was no one left to produce copper. Even at the height of the age, disaster was never far away.

[v: enkomi ruins]
On the east coast of the island stood Enkomi, a wealthy port city with streets laid out in a grid, fine houses built of cut stone, and workshops where copper was smelted and cast. Further south, near modern Larnaca, lay Hala Sultan Tekke, a large harbor town whose excavations have produced goods from across the Mediterranean world, from Sardinia to Egypt to the Baltic.

[v: cypro minoan script]
The people of Late Bronze Age Cyprus also wrote. They used a script that we call Cypro-Minoan, descended from the writing of Minoan Crete. And here is a remarkable fact. Even today, more than three thousand years later, no one can read it. We can recognize the signs. We can see that it was used on clay tablets, balls, and objects. But the language it records is still a mystery.

[v: cyprus late bronze age ruins]
When the storm arrived, Cyprus was hit hard. Around 1200 B.C.E., several sites show signs of destruction or abandonment. At a place called Pyla-Kokkinokremos, on a ridge overlooking the coast, archaeologists found a fortified settlement that seems to have been occupied only briefly, perhaps for a single generation, before it was abandoned. Hoards of metal and valuables were found buried there, never recovered by their owners.

[v: maa palaeokastro]
At Maa-Palaeokastro, on a rocky headland in the west of the island, a defended settlement with a wall across its neck was built around this time. Some archaeologists have suggested that the people who built these sites were newcomers from the Aegean, others that they were Cypriots seeking safety in a dangerous age. As with so much of this period, the evidence can be read in more than one way.

[v: enkomi ingot god]
But unlike the Hittites, unlike Ugarit, and unlike the Mycenaean palaces, Cyprus did not simply collapse and disappear. Enkomi was damaged, but rebuilt. Kition, near Larnaca, was rebuilt as well, with new temples. And it was in Enkomi, in the twelfth century, that someone made one of the most striking bronze statues of the age. A warrior god, wearing a horned helmet, holding a spear and shield, standing on a base shaped like an oxhide ingot. Scholars call him the Ingot God. The god of copper itself, standing guard over the island's wealth.

[v: horned god enkomi]
Cyprus seems to have adapted. Its copper industry continued. Its merchants found new partners. Over the following century, settlers who spoke Greek arrived on the island, and by the Iron Age, Cyprus was a land of small kingdoms where Greek was widely spoken, alongside other languages, some of which we still cannot fully understand.

[v: cyprus ancient harbor]
Hala Sultan Tekke was eventually abandoned, perhaps because its harbor was silting up, perhaps because the trade that made it rich had dried up. But Cyprus as a whole survived the collapse better than almost anywhere else in the region.

[v: cypriot pottery]
Why? Historians are still debating that question. One possibility is that Cyprus was less dependent on a single, rigid palace system than places like Pylos or Hattusa. Its wealth was spread among several towns, and its merchants were used to adapting to changing markets. In a world where the most centralized states fell the hardest, a more flexible society may have had a better chance of riding out the storm.

[v: medinet habu]
But one great power was still to face the full force of the crisis. A kingdom far older than any of the others, which had seen empires rise and fall before. Egypt.
