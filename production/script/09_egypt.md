# CHAPTER NINE: EGYPT'S LAST STAND

[v: medinet habu temple]
On the west bank of the Nile, across the river from modern Luxor, stands the memorial temple of Pharaoh Ramesses the Third, at a place called Medinet Habu. It is one of the best preserved temples in all of Egypt. Its walls are covered with colossal carved scenes, many of them still bearing traces of their original paint.

[v: ramesses iii]
Ramesses the Third came to the throne around 1184 B.C.E. He was not a descendant of Ramesses the Second, though he took his name and deliberately modeled himself on his great predecessor. He would become the last great warrior pharaoh of the New Kingdom.

[v: medinet habu inscription]
And on the walls of Medinet Habu, he left us the single most famous account of the Sea Peoples. In his eighth regnal year, which by the most widely used chronology falls around 1177 B.C.E., his scribes recorded the following words.

[v: medinet habu relief sea peoples]
The foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands. All at once the lands were removed and scattered in the fray. No land could stand before their arms, from Hatti, Kode, Carchemish, Arzawa, and Alashiya on, being cut off at one time. A camp was set up in one place in Amurru. They desolated its people, and its land was like that which has never come into being. They were coming forward toward Egypt, while the flame was prepared before them.

[v: sea peoples relief prisoners]
The inscription goes on to name the members of this confederation. The Peleset, the Tjeker, the Shekelesh, the Denyen and the Weshesh. Lands united.

[v: medinet habu naval battle relief]
Then it describes two great battles. One on land, somewhere on Egypt's border in the Levant. And one at sea, in the mouths of the Nile, where the enemy fleet was trapped. Ramesses claims that he prepared the river mouths like a strong wall, with warships, galleys and coasters, fully manned from bow to stern with brave warriors.

[v: medinet habu naval battle]
The carved scenes of this naval battle are among the most famous images to survive from the ancient world. Egyptian ships, with lion-headed prows, close in on the enemy. Egyptian archers fire from the decks and from the shore. Enemy ships, with bird-headed prows at both ends, are capsized. Their warriors, some wearing horned helmets, others wearing distinctive headdresses that look like a crown of feathers or reeds, fall into the water or are hauled out as prisoners.

[v: sea peoples ox cart relief]
But perhaps the most revealing scene is the one depicting the land battle. Among the warriors, there are ox carts carrying women and children. These were not just armies. They were entire communities on the move, looking for new land.

[v: egyptian prisoners relief]
According to the inscriptions, Egypt won. The invaders were defeated, captured, branded and, in some cases, settled in Egyptian fortresses as soldiers. Ramesses the Third saved Egypt from the fate that had befallen the Hittites and Ugarit.

[v: deir el medina]
But the cost of victory was enormous, and the decades that followed show a state under severe strain. In the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses the Third, the workmen who built the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings, living in the village we now call Deir el-Medina, went on strike because their grain rations had not been delivered. They laid down their tools and sat behind the temples, refusing to work. It is the earliest well documented labor strike in history.

[v: turin papyrus]
Then, toward the end of his reign, a conspiracy formed within the royal harem. One of the king's secondary wives, Tiye, plotted with courtiers and officials to murder Ramesses and place her son, Pentawere, on the throne. Records of the trial that followed survive on papyrus. Many conspirators were condemned to death, and some were allowed to take their own lives.

[v: ramesses iii mummy]
For a long time, it was unclear whether the plot had actually succeeded. Then, in 2012, a team of scientists published the results of CT scans of Ramesses the Third's mummy. They found a deep cut across his throat, severe enough to have killed him almost instantly, which had been hidden under the bandages by the embalmers. A later study also found that one of his toes appears to have been cut off, perhaps in the same attack. The last great pharaoh of the New Kingdom, it seems, was murdered.

[v: valley of the kings]
Egypt survived. But it was never the same. Over the following decades, under a series of weaker kings who all bore the name Ramesses, Egypt lost control of its empire in the Levant. Tomb robbing spread in the Valley of the Kings. Prices rose. Royal power faded, and the priests of Amun at Thebes grew more powerful. By around 1070 B.C.E., the New Kingdom had come to an end, and Egypt was divided.

[v: philistine pottery]
And the defeated Sea Peoples? Some of them, at least, did not disappear. One group, the Peleset, settled on the southern coast of Canaan, in cities like Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron, Gath and Gaza. We know them by another name. The Philistines.
