# CHAPTER TWO: THE SHIP AT ULUBURUN

[v: kas turkey coast]
In 1982, a young sponge diver named Mehmet Çakır was working off a rocky headland called Uluburun, near the town of Kaş in southern Turkey. At a depth of about fifty meters, he saw something strange on the seabed. He described them as metal biscuits with ears.

[v: oxhide ingot]
Archaeologists who heard his description realized exactly what he had seen. They were oxhide ingots, slabs of copper cast in a distinctive shape, with four handle-like corners, that was used to transport metal across the Late Bronze Age world.

[v: uluburun shipwreck]
Over the following decade, between 1984 and 1994, a team from the Institute of Nautical Archaeology, led first by George Bass and then by Cemal Pulak, made more than twenty thousand dives to the wreck. What they brought up was one of the most important archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century.

[v: uluburun shipwreck cargo]
The ship was small, perhaps fifteen meters long, built of cedar. It sank around 1300 B.C.E. And its cargo was a snapshot of the entire Late Bronze Age economy.

[v: copper ingots museum bodrum]
There were around ten tons of copper, mostly in the form of more than three hundred and fifty oxhide ingots. Chemical analysis suggests most of this copper came from Cyprus. There was roughly a ton of tin. Ten to one. Exactly the right ratio to make bronze. This ship was carrying enough raw material to equip a small army.

[v: glass ingot bronze age]
But that was only the beginning. There were ingots of glass, in cobalt blue and turquoise, some of them likely made in Egypt or Mesopotamia. There were large Canaanite storage jars, many filled with terebinth resin, a substance probably used in incense or perfume. There were logs of African ebony. There was elephant ivory and hippopotamus teeth, ostrich eggshells, and spices.

[v: mycenaean pottery]
There was Mycenaean pottery from Greece, Cypriot pottery, and Canaanite jewelry. There were weapons and tools whose styles point to Italy, the Balkans, the Aegean, Egypt and the Levant. There was a scarab made of gold, bearing the name of Nefertiti, the queen of Egypt and wife of Akhenaten.

[v: bronze age writing board]
And there was something small and easily overlooked. A wooden writing board, made of two leaves joined by an ivory hinge, which once held a layer of wax. Someone on board had been keeping records. We will never know what they wrote.

[v: uluburun museum bodrum]
We don't know for certain where the Uluburun ship was going, or who owned it. One popular theory is that it was sailing west from the Levant or Cyprus toward the Aegean, perhaps carrying a royal shipment, a gift from one king to another. What is clear is that a single ship could carry materials from at least seven or eight different cultures, from Africa to the Baltic, since even amber was found on board.

[v: bronze age ship model]
This was not a world of isolated kingdoms. It was a world where a palace in Greece relied on copper from Cyprus, tin from the far east, glass from Egypt and ivory from Africa. A world where any disruption in one place could ripple outward to all the others.

[v: kadesh relief ramesses chariot]
And yet, in this same century, the great powers were also preparing for war. The two greatest of them, Egypt and the Hittites, were about to meet in one of the largest chariot battles ever fought.
