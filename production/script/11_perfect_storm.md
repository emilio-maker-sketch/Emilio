# CHAPTER ELEVEN: THE PERFECT STORM

[v: drought landscape]
Over the past two decades, a new kind of evidence has transformed the debate about the end of the Bronze Age. It does not come from palaces or inscriptions. It comes from mud, from pollen, and from the rings of ancient trees.

[v: sea of galilee]
In 2013, a team of researchers led by Dafna Langgut published the results of a sediment core drilled from the bed of the Sea of Galilee. Layer by layer, the mud preserved tiny grains of pollen, a record of the plants growing in the region over thousands of years. Toward the end of the Late Bronze Age, the pollen of trees like oak, pine and olive dropped sharply. This is a sign of a much drier climate, a long stretch of drought that the researchers placed roughly between 1250 and 1100 B.C.E.

[v: larnaca salt lake cyprus]
That same year, a team led by David Kaniewski published a study of sediments from a salt lake near Larnaca, on Cyprus, close to the Bronze Age harbor city of Hala Sultan Tekke. It also pointed to a shift to drier conditions at about this time, a period of drought that seems to have lasted for as long as three centuries.

[v: juniper tree rings]
Then, in 2023, a study led by Sturt Manning, published in the journal Nature, looked at the growth rings of ancient juniper wood from Gordion, in central Anatolia, the heart of the old Hittite lands. Tree rings grow thicker in wet years and thinner in dry ones. The researchers found a severe drought in this region, and in particular three consecutive extremely dry years, dated to around 1198 to 1196 B.C.E.

[v: wheat field ancient]
Three years in a row of failed harvests, in a region where the great Hittite state already depended on imported grain. That is exactly when we see the desperate letters about grain shipments, a matter of life or death. It fits the timeline of the Hittite collapse disturbingly well.

[v: earthquake ruins]
But drought was not the only natural force at work. In 2000, the geophysicist Amos Nur and the archaeologist Eric Cline proposed what they called an earthquake storm. The eastern Mediterranean is one of the most seismically active regions in the world. Nur and Cline argued that over a period of perhaps fifty years, a sequence of large earthquakes could have struck many of the major Bronze Age sites, including Mycenae, Tiryns, Troy, Ugarit and others.

[v: collapsed stone wall archaeology]
Earthquakes alone probably would not have brought down entire civilizations. Societies have survived earthquakes throughout history, rebuilding again and again. But earthquakes striking already weakened states, at a time when their resources were stretched thin, could have been devastating.

[v: ancient revolt]
Then there are the human factors. Some scholars have suggested that internal rebellion played a part. When harvests fail and palaces keep demanding taxes and labor, ordinary people may have turned against their rulers. Some of the destroyed palaces could have been burned not by foreign invaders, but by their own subjects.

[v: bronze age trade ship]
And there is the network itself. Remember the Uluburun ship, with its copper from Cyprus and its tin from the distant east. Every palace depended on these long, fragile chains of trade. If drought hit one region, if earthquakes struck another, if pirates made the sea lanes too dangerous to sail, if a key trading city like Ugarit was destroyed, the effects would have spread through the whole system.

[v: eric cline 1177]
This is the explanation that the historian Eric Cline, whose book on the subject gave this date its fame, has championed. A systems collapse. Not a single cause, but many at once. Climate change, drought and famine. Earthquakes. Invasion and migration. Internal unrest. The breakdown of international trade. Each of these, on its own, might have been survivable. Together, they reinforced one another, in a cascade of failures that no single kingdom could stop.

[v: hittite ruins sunset]
Complex societies, as the historian Joseph Tainter has argued, can become vulnerable precisely because of their complexity. The more specialized and interconnected they are, the more their survival depends on everything working at once. The Late Bronze Age palaces were masters of complexity: central administrations, scribes, specialized workshops, and trade routes spanning thousands of kilometers. When the shocks came, they had a very long way to fall.

[v: ancient ruins collapse]
It is important to be careful here. Not every region collapsed at the same time or in the same way. Some places, like Cyprus, recovered relatively quickly. Assyria survived, though weakened. Egypt held on. The collapse was not one event on one day, but a process unfolding over several decades, with different causes mattering more in different places.

[v: bronze age map]
But the overall pattern is unmistakable. In a few generations, the world of the great kings, of the Amarna letters, of brother writing to brother, was gone.
