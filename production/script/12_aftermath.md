# CHAPTER TWELVE: OUT OF THE RUINS

[v: iron age]
Historians often describe what came next as a dark age. And in many places, it was. Populations fell. Cities shrank or disappeared. Long-distance trade dwindled. Writing vanished from Greece. Monumental art and architecture largely stopped.

[v: iron sword ancient]
But collapse is never only an ending. Out of the ruins of the Bronze Age, a new world slowly emerged, and it would turn out to be the world that shaped our own.

[v: iron age weapons]
The first great change was a new metal. Iron had been known and worked for centuries, but it had been a rare and precious material. As the trade in tin broke down, and as smiths learned better ways of working iron, it gradually became the common metal of tools and weapons. Unlike copper and tin, iron ore is found almost everywhere. You did not need a palace, a fleet or a network of foreign kings to obtain it. In a sense, iron democratized metal.

[v: phoenician ship]
The second great change came from the coast of the Levant. Some of the old Canaanite cities, like Byblos, Sidon and Tyre, survived the collapse, or recovered quickly from it. Their people, whom the Greeks would later call the Phoenicians, became the great seafaring traders of the Iron Age. Their ships sailed farther than any Bronze Age merchants had ever gone, all the way across the Mediterranean, and eventually beyond the Strait of Gibraltar. They founded colonies, including, according to tradition, the city of Carthage.

[v: phoenician alphabet inscription]
And they spread something that would change the world forever. The alphabet. The Phoenician alphabet, a simple set of twenty-two letters, could be learned in days rather than years. Writing was no longer the secret skill of palace scribes. In the eighth century B.C.E., the Greeks adopted it, adding letters for vowels. From the Greek alphabet came the Etruscan, then the Latin alphabet, the one you are reading on your screen at this moment.

[v: kingdom of israel]
In the southern Levant, in the space left by the retreat of Egypt and the fall of the Canaanite city-states, new peoples and new kingdoms emerged. The Philistines on the coast. The kingdoms of Israel and Judah in the highlands. Aram, Ammon, Moab and Edom. Many of the stories of the Hebrew Bible are set in this new Iron Age world.

[v: neo assyrian relief]
In Mesopotamia, Assyria survived its own crises and, a few centuries later, rose to become a new kind of empire, larger and more ruthless than anything the Bronze Age had seen.

[v: homer bust]
And in Greece, after centuries of silence, a new civilization would rise, one of independent city-states, of the Olympic games, of philosophy, democracy and drama. But they never forgot the age that had come before. Their poets sang of a lost age of heroes, of mighty kings who lived in golden Mycenae, and of a great war fought at Troy. The Iliad and the Odyssey, the founding works of Western literature, are in part a distant memory of the Bronze Age world that fell.

[v: mycenae ruins]
So what really killed the great civilizations of the Late Bronze Age? Not a single invasion. Not a single catastrophe. The evidence points to a deadly combination. Years of drought and famine. Earthquakes. Rebellion from within. Peoples uprooted and on the move. And a tightly connected international system that turned each local disaster into a shared one.

[v: ugarit ruins sunset]
Perhaps that is why this story feels so relevant today. The world of 1177 B.C.E. was one of interconnected economies, of supply chains stretching across continents, of rulers who believed their prosperity would last forever. It was a world that seemed too big, too rich and too connected to fail.

[v: ancient tablet]
And when it did fail, the last king of Ugarit wrote to his father in Cyprus, asking for help that would never come.
