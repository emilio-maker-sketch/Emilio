# CHAPTER FOUR: THE FIRST CRACKS

[v: merneptah]
When Ramesses the Second died, he was succeeded by his thirteenth son, Merneptah, already an old man by the standards of his time. And within five years of taking the throne, Merneptah faced an invasion.

[v: libyan egyptian relief]
Around 1208 B.C.E., a Libyan chief named Meryey led a great force east toward the fertile land of the Nile Delta. According to Merneptah's inscription at Karnak, they came with their wives, their children and their cattle. They were not simply raiders. They were looking for a place to live.

[v: karnak inscription]
And they did not come alone. Among their allies, the Egyptian scribes listed five peoples who came, in the words of the inscription, from the lands of the sea. The Ekwesh. The Teresh. The Lukka. The Sherden. And the Shekelesh.

[v: sherden warriors relief]
This is one of the first times we meet the people that modern historians call the Sea Peoples. And it is important to notice something right away. Some of these names were not new. The Lukka were known from Hittite texts as the people of a rugged coastal region in southwestern Anatolia, a land of pirates and rebels. The Sherden had already appeared generations earlier, both as raiders and as mercenaries, some of whom fought in Pharaoh's own army.

[v: merneptah stele cairo]
Merneptah won. His inscriptions boast of thousands of enemy dead, and of the hands and other body parts cut from the slain and counted by his scribes. He celebrated his victory with a stone monument now known as the Merneptah Stele, which includes, almost in passing, the earliest known mention of a people called Israel.

[v: wheat ancient egypt agriculture]
But there is another line in Merneptah's inscriptions that is easy to miss, and it may be one of the most important clues of all. The pharaoh says that he sent grain in ships, to keep the land of Hatti alive.

[v: hattusa ruins]
Think about that for a moment. The Hittite Empire was one of the two superpowers of its age. And yet here it was, apparently dependent on grain shipments from Egypt to feed its people.

[v: hittite tablet]
The Hittite records themselves confirm the picture. Letters from the late thirteenth century speak again and again about grain. One letter, sent from the Hittite court to the king of Ugarit, demands that a ship and crew be provided to carry two thousand measures of grain to the Hittite port of Ura. It ends with a phrase that has echoed through modern scholarship. It is a matter of life or death.

[v: ugarit ruins]
Another letter, found at Ugarit, appears to scold its king for not having sent supplies quickly enough, and asks, in effect, whether he does not know that there is famine in the land. Something was going badly wrong in the agricultural heartlands of Anatolia.

[v: assyrian relief]
At the same time, the political order was shifting. In the east, the Assyrian king Tukulti-Ninurta the First defeated a Hittite army, and later sacked Babylon, carrying away the statue of its chief god, Marduk. The old balance of power between the great kings was breaking apart.

[v: hittite king relief]
In the Hittite capital, the last great kings, Tudhaliya the Fourth and then Suppiluliuma the Second, faced threats on every side: rival branches of their own royal family, rebellious vassals in western Anatolia, and new enemies on the sea.

[v: hattusa sudburg hieroglyphic inscription]
Suppiluliuma the Second left an inscription that records something quite unusual for a land-based empire like the Hittites. A naval battle. He claims to have fought the ships of Alashiya, of Cyprus, three times on the sea, and to have destroyed them. Who exactly was crewing those ships, and whether Cyprus itself was the enemy or had been taken over by others, we do not know.

[v: bronze age warship]
What we can see is a world under rising stress. Hunger in Anatolia. Peoples on the move along the coasts of North Africa and the Aegean. Rivalries among the great kings. A sea that was becoming more dangerous with every passing year.

[v: ugarit royal palace]
And at the center of all this, on the Syrian coast, sat the richest trading city of them all. A city that had no idea it was about to become the most famous casualty of the age.
