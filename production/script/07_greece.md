# CHAPTER SEVEN: THE FALL OF THE PALACES OF GREECE

[v: mycenae citadel]
On a hill in the northeastern Peloponnese, overlooking the plain of Argos, stands the citadel of Mycenae. Its walls are built of stones so massive that later Greeks believed they could only have been lifted by the Cyclopes, the one-eyed giants of myth. We still call this style of building Cyclopean masonry.

[v: lion gate mycenae]
You enter Mycenae through the Lion Gate, built around 1250 B.C.E., with two great stone lions, or perhaps lionesses, carved above the doorway. It is the oldest monumental sculpture in Europe that still stands in its original place.

[v: mask of agamemnon]
In 1876, the German businessman and excavator Heinrich Schliemann dug into a circle of royal shaft graves just inside the gate. He found a staggering treasure of gold. Diadems, cups, jewelry, weapons, and a set of gold funeral masks. Over one of them, he is said to have announced that he had gazed upon the face of Agamemnon, the legendary king of Mycenae who led the Greeks to Troy. In fact, the graves were centuries older than any Trojan War could have been. But the name stuck. It is still called the Mask of Agamemnon.

[v: mycenaean palace fresco]
By the thirteenth century B.C.E., Mycenaean Greece was a world of rival palace states. Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes, and others. Each palace was the center of a highly organized economy. Scribes recorded, in great detail, the land, livestock, workers, raw materials and finished goods that the palace controlled.

[v: linear b tablet]
They wrote in a script we call Linear B. For decades, nobody could read it. Then, in 1952, an English architect named Michael Ventris, working with the philologist John Chadwick, showed that it was an early form of Greek. Suddenly, the administrative records of the Mycenaean palaces could speak.

[v: palace of nestor pylos]
The most important collection of these records came from the palace at Pylos in southwestern Greece, which the American archaeologist Carl Blegen discovered in 1939, on the very first day of excavation. He found more than a thousand Linear B tablets there.

[v: pylos throne room]
Here is the strange thing. Linear B tablets were not meant to last. They were made of unbaked clay, like temporary notes, and were regularly recycled. The only reason we have them at all is that the palace of Pylos burned down, around 1180 B.C.E., and the fire baked the clay hard. The disaster that destroyed the palace is what preserved its final records.

[v: linear b tablet pylos]
And those final records are fascinating. Some tablets list rowers being sent to a place called Pleuron. Others describe groups of men posted along the coast, watchers, or guards of the coastline, as some scholars have interpreted them. Some historians have read these texts as emergency measures, a kingdom preparing for an attack from the sea. Others caution that these could simply be routine. Either way, they offer a tantalizing glimpse of the last weeks of a Bronze Age palace.

[v: tiryns walls]
Pylos was not alone. Across Greece, between roughly 1225 and 1175 B.C.E., the great palace centers were damaged or destroyed. Mycenae suffered destruction. Tiryns, with its massive walls, was damaged. Thebes burned. Some sites were rebuilt for a time on a smaller scale. Others were abandoned entirely.

[v: tiryns citadel]
At Tiryns, excavators have found evidence that has been interpreted as earthquake damage, including collapsed walls and, in one case, skeletons that may have been crushed beneath falling stone. At Mycenae too, there are signs that have been linked to seismic activity. We will return to the question of earthquakes later.

[v: mycenaean warrior vase]
After the palaces fell, life in Greece did not end. People continued to live in many places. In fact, for a few generations, some communities even seem to have done reasonably well. A famous painted vessel from Mycenae, called the Warrior Vase, shows a line of armed soldiers marching off, while a woman raises her arm, perhaps in farewell. It is often dated to this period, just after the destruction of the palaces.

[v: greek dark age pottery]
But the palace system, with its scribes, its central storerooms and its long-distance trade, was gone for good. Within a few generations, population levels across much of Greece appear to have dropped dramatically. Many settlements were abandoned. Monumental building stopped. And Linear B, the script of the palaces, vanished completely.

[v: geometric greek pottery]
For roughly four centuries, the Greeks lived in a world without writing, a period historians long called the Greek Dark Age. When writing finally returned, in the eighth century B.C.E., it came in an entirely new form: an alphabet borrowed from the Phoenicians. The memory of the palaces survived only in myth, in stories of heroes, kings and a great war against Troy, eventually set down in the poems of Homer.

[v: troy ruins]
And Troy itself, on the coast of northwestern Anatolia, was also destroyed in this period, around 1180 B.C.E., in the layer archaeologists call Troy Seven A. Whether there was ever a real Trojan War, and whether this destruction had anything to do with it, remains one of the great debates of ancient history.

[v: egypt nile]
In the east, the great empires were gone. In the west, the palaces of Greece had fallen. One great power remained standing. But between Greece and Egypt lay an island that would face the storm in its own way.
