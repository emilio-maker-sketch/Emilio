# CHAPTER TEN: WHO WERE THE SEA PEOPLES?

[v: sea peoples relief medinet habu]
For more than a century, the Sea Peoples have been the great villains of the Bronze Age collapse. In older history books, they appear as a kind of barbarian horde, sweeping across the eastern Mediterranean, burning everything in their path, and bringing civilization to its knees.

[v: gaston maspero]
The very name Sea Peoples is a modern invention. It was popularized in the nineteenth century by French Egyptologists, especially Gaston Maspero, who drew on phrases in the Egyptian inscriptions about peoples from the sea and from their islands. The Egyptians themselves never used it as a single label for one nation.

[v: sea peoples warriors]
So who were they really? The honest answer is that we are still not certain. But we can say a few things with some confidence.

[v: sea peoples prisoners medinet habu]
First, they were not one people. They were a shifting collection of groups with different names, different dress, and probably different origins. Some had been known in the region for generations as pirates, raiders or mercenaries. The Sherden, for example, served as bodyguards to Ramesses the Second at Kadesh.

[v: sardinia nuraghe]
Second, scholars have long tried to link their names to places. The Sherden have been connected with Sardinia, and the Shekelesh with Sicily. The Lukka almost certainly came from Lycia in southwestern Anatolia. The Denyen have been linked to the Danaoi, one of Homer's names for the Greeks, and the Ekwesh to the Achaeans, another. The Teresh have been connected, much more speculatively, with the Tyrsenians, a name later Greeks used for the Etruscans. Some of these connections are well supported. Others rest on little more than a similarity of sound. And even where a name matches, it is often unclear whether these peoples came from those places, or went there later, after the collapse.

[v: philistine pottery bichrome]
Third, and most importantly, the archaeology of the Philistines gives us a rare, direct glimpse of at least one of these groups. In the early twelfth century B.C.E., at sites like Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron and Gath, a new material culture suddenly appears. Locally made pottery painted in a style that closely resembles Mycenaean pottery from the Aegean. Hearths of an Aegean type. Changes in diet, including more pork. Even new kinds of loom weights for weaving.

[v: ashkelon excavation]
In 2019, a team of geneticists and archaeologists published a study of ancient DNA from skeletons buried at Ashkelon. They found that people buried there in the early Iron Age, right after the Philistines arrived, carried a distinct genetic component that pointed toward southern Europe, a component that was not present in the earlier Bronze Age population. Within a couple of centuries, that signal had been largely diluted by intermarriage with the local Levantine population.

[v: philistine settlement]
In other words, at least some of the newcomers really did come from the west, across the sea. But they did not wipe out the people already living there. They settled among them, and their descendants gradually blended in.

[v: refugees ancient migration]
This changes how we should think about the Sea Peoples. Instead of a horde of destroyers who caused the collapse, many historians now see them, at least in part, as a symptom of it. People displaced by hunger, war and the breakdown of their own societies, taking to the sea in search of somewhere new to live. Some of them certainly raided and burned. Some fought as mercenaries for whoever would pay. Some brought their families in ox carts and settled down.

[v: ancient greek ship]
And there is a deeper problem with blaming them for everything. Many of the destroyed sites show no clear sign of who destroyed them. At some, there is evidence of fire but no weapons. At others, there are signs of earthquakes. And at some, like Hattusa, the evidence points more toward abandonment than attack. The Sea Peoples were real. They were dangerous. But they cannot explain everything on their own.

[v: drought cracked earth]
To understand why so many great civilizations fell at once, we have to look beyond the soldiers and ships, and into the soil, the water and the sky.
