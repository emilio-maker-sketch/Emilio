# CHAPTER ONE: A WORLD OF BROTHERS

[v: amarna letter tablet]
In 1887, a local woman digging for fertile soil in the ruins at Amarna, in Middle Egypt, is said to have found a cache of clay tablets. They were covered not in hieroglyphs, but in cuneiform, the wedge-shaped script of Mesopotamia. Many were broken or lost as they passed through the hands of dealers, but in the end around three hundred and eighty of them survived. Today we call them the Amarna Letters.

[v: akhenaten]
They turned out to be the diplomatic archive of the Egyptian court in the fourteenth century B.C.E., during the reigns of Amenhotep the Third and his son Akhenaten. And what they reveal is astonishing. They show a world of great kings who wrote to each other constantly, in a shared diplomatic language, Akkadian, and who called each other by a single word. Brother.

[v: amenhotep iii statue]
There was the king of Egypt, the richest of them all, whose land was famous for its gold. There was the king of Babylon, ruled at the time by a Kassite dynasty. There was the king of Mitanni, a powerful state in northern Mesopotamia and Syria. Later there would be the king of Assyria, rising fast. And to the north, in the highlands of Anatolia, there was the king of Hatti, ruler of the Hittites.

[v: hittite sculpture]
These rulers formed something that historians have compared to a club of great powers. They exchanged ambassadors and gifts. They married each other's daughters. They argued, they sulked, and they complained, constantly, about not being treated with enough respect.

[v: amarna letter]
One Babylonian king, Burna-Buriash, grumbled to Pharaoh that the gold he had been sent, when it was put in the furnace, turned out to be much less than its stated weight. Tushratta, king of Mitanni, pleaded again and again for statues of solid gold that he believed had been promised to him, and he was bitterly disappointed when they arrived plated in gold over wood. These were not the letters of distant strangers. They were the letters of a family, with all the jealousies that come with one.

[v: minoan fresco]
Beyond this inner circle lay the wider world they traded with. On Crete, the palace culture of the Minoans had flourished for centuries, though by this time it had come under the control of the mainland Greeks. On mainland Greece itself, warrior kings ruled from fortified palaces at places like Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos and Thebes. Today we call them the Mycenaeans. The Hittites seem to have called them the Ahhiyawa, and the Egyptians may have known their land as Tanaja.

[v: cyprus copper oxhide ingot]
On the island of Cyprus, known in the texts as Alashiya, lay the great copper mines of the Mediterranean. And along the coast of the Levant were the trading cities of Canaan, like Byblos, Sidon, Tyre and Ugarit, which acted as the great middlemen between the sea and the empires of the interior.

[v: egyptian tomb painting foreigners tribute]
In tomb paintings in Thebes, Egyptian officials proudly showed foreign envoys arriving with their goods. Men from Keftiu, probably Crete, carrying elaborate metal vessels. Syrians bringing horses and chariots. Nubians bringing gold, ebony and animal skins. The world, as the Egyptians saw it, came to them.

[v: bronze dagger mycenae]
Why did all of this matter so much? Because of a single material. Bronze.

[v: bronze age tools weapons]
Bronze is an alloy, made mostly of copper with around ten percent tin. It is harder than copper alone, and it was the metal of weapons, armor, tools and prestige across the entire region. But here was the problem. Copper could be found in a number of places, most famously Cyprus. Tin was much rarer. Some of the tin used in the eastern Mediterranean seems to have come from as far away as Central Asia, carried overland through Mesopotamia, or from other distant sources whose exact location is still debated.

[v: bronze age trade routes map]
That means that every bronze sword in Mycenae, every chariot fitting in Hattusa, every tool in Egypt depended on a chain of trade that crossed seas, deserts and mountains, and passed through the hands of many different peoples. No single kingdom could make bronze alone. They needed each other.

[v: bronze age mediterranean map]
This was, in a very real sense, the first globalized economy in the Mediterranean world. Long before Rome, long before classical Athens, there was a network of kingdoms bound together by trade, diplomacy and marriage. And like every globalized economy, its great strength was also its great weakness.

[v: shipwreck underwater archaeology]
To understand just how connected this world was, we need to go to the bottom of the sea, off the southern coast of Turkey, where a ship went down more than three thousand three hundred years ago.
