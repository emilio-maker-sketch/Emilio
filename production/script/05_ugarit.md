# CHAPTER FIVE: THE LAST DAYS OF UGARIT

[v: ras shamra ugarit]
In 1928, a farmer ploughing his field near the Syrian coast, at a place called Minet el-Beida, struck a stone slab. Beneath it was an ancient tomb. The next year, French archaeologists led by Claude Schaeffer began digging at the large mound nearby, called Ras Shamra. They soon realized that they had found Ugarit, a city known until then only from mentions in the Amarna letters and other foreign texts.

[v: ugarit palace ruins]
Excavations have continued, on and off, for most of a century since. What they have revealed is a city of perhaps six to eight thousand people, with a great royal palace of around ninety rooms, temples to the gods Baal and Dagan, wealthy private houses, and an adjoining harbor that linked it to Cyprus, Egypt, Anatolia and the Aegean.

[v: ugaritic alphabet tablet]
But Ugarit's greatest treasure was not gold. It was words. Archaeologists have found thousands of clay tablets there, written in more than half a dozen languages and several different scripts. Among them was something extraordinary: an alphabet of thirty letters, written in cuneiform wedges. One small tablet found there even lists the letters in order, making it one of the oldest known alphabet primers in the world.

[v: ugaritic tablet louvre]
The texts written in that alphabet, in the local Ugaritic language, include myths and epic poems about the god Baal, his battles with the sea god Yam and with Mot, the god of death. Scholars of the Hebrew Bible have studied them closely, because they share imagery and phrases with some of the oldest passages of the Bible.

[v: akkadian tablet]
And then there are the letters. Thousands of them, preserved in the palace and in the houses of officials and merchants. Through them, we can follow Ugarit's final years almost as if we were reading its mail.

[v: hittite seal ugarit]
Ugarit was a vassal of the Hittites. Its kings owed loyalty, tribute, and military service to the great king in Hattusa. And in its final years, the demands from the Hittite court were relentless. Send grain. Send ships. Send troops.

[v: ancient ship relief]
Then the letters start to change. One letter to the king of Ugarit from the king of Cyprus warns him about enemy ships, telling him to fortify his cities, bring his troops and chariots inside the walls, and wait for the enemy with strength. Another letter from a Hittite official speaks of a people called the Shikala, who live on ships, and asks for a man who had been held captive by them to be sent for questioning.

[v: ugarit tablet letter]
And then there is the letter we heard at the very beginning of this story. Ammurapi, Ugarit's last king, writes to the king of Cyprus, whom he addresses respectfully as his father. The enemy's ships have come. His cities have been burned. His army is away in the land of the Hittites. His fleet is away in the land of Lukka. Seven enemy ships have done terrible damage.

[v: ugarit destruction layer]
Here is where the story has often been told with a powerful twist. For decades, it was said that some of these final letters were found inside a kiln, where they had been placed to be baked hard before being sent, and that the city fell before they could ever be dispatched. It is a haunting image. Unfortunately, more recent reexamination of the excavation records suggests that it is probably not true. The tablets seem to have fallen from an upper story when the building collapsed, and some were found in a different place than the story claims.

[v: ugarit ruins stone walls]
But the destruction itself is very real. Archaeologists found Ugarit burned, with collapsed walls and a thick layer of ash. Arrowheads were scattered through the streets of the city. Hoards of valuables had been buried and never recovered, as if their owners hoped to come back for them, and never did.

[v: egyptian official bay]
We can even date the end of Ugarit fairly closely, thanks to another letter found in its ruins. It was sent by an Egyptian official named Bay, a powerful chancellor in the Egyptian court. We know from Egyptian records that Bay was executed in the fifth year of Pharaoh Siptah, around 1192 B.C.E. Since his letter reached Ugarit while he was still alive, the city must have still been standing then. Most scholars now place its destruction in the years shortly after, around 1190 to 1185 B.C.E.

[v: ras shamra tell]
After that, Ugarit was never rebuilt. The site remained empty for centuries. Its alphabet, its literature and its very name disappeared from the world, until a farmer's plough struck stone more than three thousand years later.

[v: hattusa lion gate]
Ugarit's overlord, the great Hittite Empire, did not long outlive it. But what happened at the Hittite capital is very different from what you might expect.
