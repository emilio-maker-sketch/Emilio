# CHAPTER THREE: KADESH AND THE FIRST PEACE

[v: ramesses ii statue]
In the spring of 1274 B.C.E., the young pharaoh Ramesses the Second marched north out of Egypt at the head of a vast army. He was in the fifth year of his reign. His target was the city of Kadesh, on the Orontes River in what is now western Syria, a strategic stronghold that Egypt and the Hittites had fought over for generations.

[v: hittite chariot relief]
Waiting for him was the Hittite king, Muwatalli the Second, with an army that, according to the Egyptian accounts, included thousands of chariots gathered from all across the Hittite world and its allies.

[v: abu simbel kadesh relief]
What happened next is one of the best documented battles of the ancient world, because Ramesses made sure it would be. He had the story carved on the walls of temples across Egypt, at Abu Simbel, at Karnak, at Luxor, at Abydos and at his memorial temple, the Ramesseum.

[v: kadesh battle relief]
According to his version, two Bedouin men approached the Egyptian camp and claimed that the Hittite army was far away, to the north. Ramesses believed them, and pushed ahead with only part of his forces. But the men were Hittite spies. The Hittite army was hidden just behind the old city of Kadesh. Their chariots smashed into one of the Egyptian divisions as it marched, scattered it, and then crashed into the pharaoh's camp.

[v: ramesses ii chariot relief]
In the Egyptian telling, Ramesses was abandoned by almost everyone, and fought on alone, calling on the god Amun, charging the enemy again and again until the battle turned. Reinforcements arrived just in time, and the Hittite attack collapsed.

[v: karnak temple]
It is a magnificent story. It is also, almost certainly, propaganda. When we look at what happened afterward, the picture changes. Ramesses did not take Kadesh. He returned to Egypt. And the Hittites pushed further south, taking territory that had belonged to Egypt's sphere of influence. At best, the battle was a bloody draw. Hittite sources, for their part, seem to have regarded it as a victory.

[v: hittite peace treaty tablet]
But the most remarkable thing about Kadesh is what came after. About fifteen years later, around 1259 B.C.E., Ramesses and a new Hittite king, Hattusili the Third, did something that had never been done in quite this way before. They signed a formal, written peace treaty.

[v: hattusili treaty istanbul museum]
We have both versions. The Egyptian copy was carved in hieroglyphs on the walls of Karnak and the Ramesseum. The Hittite copy was written in Akkadian on a clay tablet, found in the ruins of the Hittite capital, Hattusa. In it, the two kings swore eternal peace and brotherhood, promised to come to each other's aid against enemies, and agreed to return political refugees to one another.

[v: united nations headquarters]
It is often called the oldest surviving peace treaty in the world. A replica of the Hittite tablet now hangs in the United Nations headquarters in New York.

[v: ramesses ii abu simbel]
The peace held. Ramesses later married a Hittite princess, who took the Egyptian name Maathorneferure. Letters flowed back and forth between the royal families. Hittite queen Puduhepa wrote directly to Ramesses, and Ramesses wrote back.

[v: abu simbel temple]
Ramesses the Second would go on to rule for sixty-six years. He outlived many of his own children. By the time he died, around 1213 B.C.E., he had become the very image of an eternal pharaoh. To most people in Egypt, it must have seemed that the world he ruled would last forever.

[v: merneptah stele]
It would not. Within a single lifetime of his death, the whole system that he and his Hittite brothers had built would be in ruins. And the first warning signs had already begun to appear.
