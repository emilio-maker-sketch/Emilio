# CHAPTER SIX: THE EMPTY CAPITAL

[v: hattusa]
High on the central Anatolian plateau, about one hundred and fifty kilometers east of modern Ankara, lie the ruins of Hattusa, the capital of the Hittite Empire. At its height, it was one of the greatest cities in the world. Its walls ran for more than six kilometers, studded with towers, pierced by monumental gates guarded by carved lions, sphinxes, and a warrior god.

[v: hattusa great temple]
Inside were temples, palaces, grain silos and archives holding tens of thousands of clay tablets. The Hittites were meticulous record keepers. They wrote treaties, laws, rituals, prayers, letters and royal annals in a variety of languages, chief among them Hittite itself, the oldest written Indo-European language we know.

[v: hattusa grain silos]
The Hittites also thought carefully about food. At Hattusa, archaeologists have found enormous underground grain silos, one complex of which could have held several thousand tons of grain, enough to feed many thousands of people for a year. These silos are a reminder that the Hittite state understood how vulnerable it was to bad harvests. And yet, as we have seen, by the end of the thirteenth century it was relying on grain from abroad.

[v: hattusa lion gate]
For a long time, scholars imagined the end of Hattusa as a violent catastrophe. Enemies storming the walls, the great city burning, the last Hittite king dying in its defense. Fire certainly struck the city. Parts of the citadel and some of the temples show clear signs of burning.

[v: hattusa ruins archaeology]
But the German archaeologists who have excavated the site for more than a century have noticed something strange. When they dug into the palace and temple quarters, they found remarkably little left behind. Few valuables. Few objects of everyday use. Few bodies. Buildings that look as if they had been emptied before they were burned.

[v: hattusa royal citadel buyukkale]
Jürgen Seeher, who directed the excavations for many years, argued that Hattusa was not sacked by a sudden invasion. Instead, he suggested, the royal court abandoned it, packing up its treasures and its essential records and leaving for somewhere else, perhaps somewhere easier to supply or defend. The fires may have come later, set by local people or by squatters, after the great king and his court were already gone.

[v: hittite king suppiluliuma]
That makes the end of the Hittite Empire one of the most mysterious episodes of the entire collapse. We know the name of its last known king, Suppiluliuma the Second. We know the empire was under severe stress from famine, from rivals within the royal family, and from enemies around its borders. But we do not know exactly what happened to Suppiluliuma or his court. Sometime around 1180 B.C.E., the written record of the great kings of Hatti simply stops.

[v: carchemish]
The Hittite name did not disappear entirely. In the south, in cities like Carchemish on the Euphrates, branches of the royal family survived. Over the following centuries, a group of small kingdoms in southern Anatolia and northern Syria preserved Hittite traditions, royal names and art. The Bible calls their peoples Hittites, and Assyrian records called the region the land of Hatti. Scholars today call them the Neo-Hittite states.

[v: neo hittite relief]
But the great empire, the empire that had matched Egypt at Kadesh, that had held a peace treaty with Ramesses, that had ruled most of Anatolia and much of Syria for centuries, was gone.

[v: hattusa landscape]
And its disappearance was so complete that for nearly three thousand years, the Hittites were almost forgotten. Their language was lost. Their capital became a set of ruins on a hillside. It was only in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries that scholars began to rediscover them, and only in 1915 that the Czech scholar Bedřich Hrozný showed that their language could be read.

[v: lion gate mycenae]
Meanwhile, far to the west, across the Aegean Sea, another world of palaces was meeting its own end.
