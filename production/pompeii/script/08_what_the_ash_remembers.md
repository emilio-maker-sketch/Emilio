# Chapter Eight — What the Ash Remembers

[card: CHAPTER EIGHT | What the Ash Remembers]

[mood: calm]
Today, about two-thirds of Pompeii has been uncovered. Walking its streets, you can step into bakeries and bars, houses and temples, theatres and brothels. Millions of people visit it every year. And yet, after nearly three centuries of digging, the city is still surprising us.

[v: Pompeii fresco ; Villa of the Mysteries fresco]
In recent years, a new generation of excavations has revealed whole rooms of brilliantly coloured frescoes, a richly decorated shrine, a street-food counter painted with ducks and a rooster, and a black-walled banqueting hall painted with scenes from the Trojan War. Each new room adds a little more detail to the picture of the city's final years.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Pompeii body cast]
[date: AD 2020]
[mood: dark]
In a villa just outside the walls, at a place called Civita Giuliana, archaeologists found the remains of two men who had died together as the final surge struck. One was older and well dressed. The other was younger, with compressed vertebrae suggesting years of heavy labour. He may have been enslaved. Using Fiorelli's technique, the excavators made new casts of their bodies. In the same villa, they found a ceremonial chariot, almost intact, and a tiny room where an enslaved family had lived, with three beds, a chamber pot, and a chest.

[v: Pompeii body cast ; Garden of the Fugitives Pompeii]
Science is also changing how we understand the victims themselves. In two thousand and twenty-four, researchers extracted ancient DNA from the bones inside several of the plaster casts. The results overturned stories that had been told for generations. An adult wearing a gold bracelet, long believed to be a mother holding her child, turned out to be an unrelated man. A pair thought to be sisters, or a mother and daughter, included at least one male. The stories we tell about the past, it turned out, had often said more about our own assumptions than about the people of Pompeii.

[v: Pompeii surgical instruments ; Pompeii jewellery gold]
Studies of the skeletons tell us about their health, their diets, their work and their origins. Many of the people of Pompeii and Herculaneum had ancestors from the eastern Mediterranean, a reminder of how connected, and how diverse, the Roman world really was.

[v: Herculaneum papyrus scroll ; Villa of the Papyri]
[mood: rising]
And then there are the scrolls. For more than two and a half centuries, scholars tried to unroll the charred papyri from the Villa of the Papyri. Most attempts destroyed the scrolls they were meant to save. Of the hundreds that survived, only some could be read, mostly works of an obscure Greek philosopher named Philodemus.

[v: Herculaneum papyrus scroll]
[date: AD 2023]
In twenty twenty-three, an international competition called the Vesuvius Challenge invited researchers to read the scrolls without opening them. Using high-energy X-ray scans and artificial intelligence trained to detect the faint traces of ink inside the layers, a group of young researchers revealed the first word ever read from inside an unopened Herculaneum scroll. The word was porphyras: purple.

[v: Herculaneum papyrus scroll ; Villa of the Papyri]
Within months, they had recovered whole columns of text, a passage of philosophy about pleasure, music and food that had not been read by anyone for almost two thousand years. Hundreds of scrolls remain to be scanned. Some scholars believe that parts of the villa's library may still lie buried, unexcavated, beneath the ash. The lost works of ancient literature may be waiting there, in the dark.

[map: today]
[mood: tense]
But the story of Vesuvius is not only about the past. Today, around six hundred thousand people live in the so-called red zone around the volcano, the area most at risk from pyroclastic currents. Nearly three million people live in the wider metropolitan area of Naples. It is one of the most densely populated volcanic regions on Earth.

[v: Naples skyline Vesuvius night ; Naples Vesuvius panorama]
Vesuvius is one of the most closely monitored volcanoes in the world. Seismometers, satellites and gas sensors watch it day and night. Italy has an evacuation plan designed to move hundreds of thousands of people out of the danger zone in the days before an eruption. But the plan depends on getting enough warning, and on people heeding it.

[v: Vesuvius crater ; Vesuvius aerial]
Vesuvius has now been quiet for more than eighty years. The longer it rests, many volcanologists warn, the more pressure may build beneath it, and the more violent its next eruption could be.

[v: Pompeii street ; Via dell'Abbondanza Pompeii]
[mood: calm]
The people of Pompeii had no way of knowing what the mountain above them could do. They saw its vineyards, not its fire. They felt the earthquakes and thought them ordinary. When the cloud rose above the mountain, they had no word for it, no science to explain it, and no plan to escape it.

[v: Pompeii graffiti ; Pompeii fresco portrait Terentius Neo]
We are luckier. We have the science. We have the warning. And we have Pompeii itself: a city frozen in its final hours, its bread in the ovens, its slogans on the walls, its people caught in the act of living.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Garden of the Fugitives Pompeii]
[mood: dark]
In destroying Pompeii, Vesuvius gave it a strange kind of immortality. No other city from the ancient world speaks to us so directly. We know the names of its candidates, its bakers, its gladiators and its lovers. We know what they ate, what they joked about, and how they died.

[v: Pompeii forum Vesuvius ; Mount Vesuvius from Pompeii]
And above it all, still, stands the mountain. Green again, quiet again. Waiting.
