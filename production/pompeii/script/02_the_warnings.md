# Chapter Two — The Warnings

[date: 5 FEBRUARY, AD 62]
[mood: tense]
On the fifth of February in the year sixty-two, seventeen years before the eruption, the ground beneath the Bay of Naples began to shake. It was a powerful earthquake, perhaps magnitude five or six, and its centre lay close to Pompeii.

[v: Seneca bust]
The Roman philosopher Seneca, who was writing a book about natural phenomena at the time, described what happened. Pompeii, he wrote, had been laid low by an earthquake. Part of Herculaneum had collapsed, and the buildings that still stood were unsafe. A flock of six hundred sheep was killed, apparently by poisonous gases rising from the ground. Statues split apart. Some people, he said, lost their minds with terror and wandered the countryside.

[v: Pompeii earthquake relief Lararium Caecilius Iucundus ; Pompeii Temple of Apollo]
In Pompeii, we can still see the event through the eyes of the people who lived through it. In the house of a wealthy banker named Lucius Caecilius Iucundus, archaeologists found a carved marble relief on the household shrine. It shows the temple of Jupiter in the forum tilting sideways, an equestrian statue toppling, and an altar with a sacrifice being offered, perhaps a prayer of thanks for survival.

[v: Pompeii forum Vesuvius ; Pompeii Basilica]
The damage was enormous. Temples, houses, the water system, and public buildings all needed repair. Some wealthy families seem to have left the city altogether, and their grand homes were turned into workshops and laundries. Others stayed and rebuilt.

[v: Pompeii ruins panorama ; Pompeii street]
When the excavators began uncovering Pompeii centuries later, they found the city in the middle of construction. The temple of Venus was being rebuilt. Piles of building materials lay in the streets. The water supply had been partly cut off while new pipes were laid. Seventeen years after the earthquake, Pompeii was still a building site.

[v: Vesuvius crater ; Mount Vesuvius from Pompeii]
[mood: dark]
What nobody understood was that the earthquake was not a random disaster. It was almost certainly a sign that the volcano was waking up. Deep beneath Vesuvius, molten rock was rising, cracking the earth's crust as it forced its way upward.

[v: Vesuvius aerial ; Naples Vesuvius panorama]
To modern eyes, Vesuvius looks unmistakably like a volcano. But in the first century, the mountain had not erupted in living memory, or in the memory of anyone's grandparents. The last major eruption had taken place centuries earlier, so long ago that it had left no trace in the written record.

[v: Joseph Wright of Derby Vesuvius ; Vesuvius eruption 1779 painting]
Some ancient writers did notice something strange about the mountain. The Greek geographer Strabo, writing in the time of the emperor Augustus, observed that the summit of Vesuvius was flat and barren, covered in ash-coloured rock that looked as if it had been eaten by fire. He suggested that the area might once have burned, and that the fires had gone out when the fuel was exhausted. He was right. But he had no idea that the fire was only sleeping.

[v: Vesuvius crater ; Vesuvius aerial]
In fact, the summit of Vesuvius was famous for a very different reason. In the year seventy-three before Christ, the rebel gladiator Spartacus and his followers had taken refuge in its crater, then covered in wild vines. When a Roman army trapped them there, the rebels twisted the vines into ropes and climbed down the cliffs to escape. To the Romans, the mountain was a place of adventure and of wine, not of fire.

[v: Volcanic ash cloud ; Vesuvius crater]
[mood: tense]
In the years after the earthquake, the region continued to tremble. Small tremors were common across Campania, so common, as one witness would later write, that people did not find them particularly alarming. Springs may have changed or dried up. Wells may have turned sour. In the days before the eruption, the shaking grew more frequent. But to the people of the bay, these were simply the familiar restless moods of the land.

[v: Pompeii mosaic fish ; Pompeii fresco]
We should not judge them too harshly. The science of volcanoes did not exist. Earthquakes were explained by winds trapped inside the earth, or by the anger of the gods. There was no reason to connect a trembling ground with a green, peaceful mountain covered in vineyards.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Pompeii body cast]
[mood: dark]
And so, when the end came, it came without anyone recognising the warnings for what they were.

[v: Seneca bust ; Pompeii peristyle garden]
There is one more puzzle about the timing of the disaster. For centuries, historians accepted that the eruption began on the twenty-fourth of August, the date given in the surviving copies of the eyewitness letters. But the evidence from the ground does not quite fit a summer day. Archaeologists found pomegranates, chestnuts and walnuts, which ripen in autumn. Wine had already been pressed and sealed in jars. Victims seem to have been wearing heavier clothing, and braziers had been lit in some houses.

[v: Pompeii graffiti ; Pompeii election inscription]
[date: 17 OCTOBER, AD 79]
Then, in two thousand and eighteen, excavators in Pompeii uncovered a charcoal inscription scribbled on a wall in a house that was being renovated. It gives a date equivalent to the seventeenth of October. Charcoal does not last long on a wall, so it was almost certainly written in the year of the eruption, just days before the end. Many scholars now believe the eruption began not in August, but on or around the twenty-fourth of October, in the year seventy-nine.

[v: Pompeii street ; Via dell'Abbondanza Pompeii]
Whatever the exact date, on that morning, the people of Pompeii woke up, went to work, and opened their shops as usual. It was the last ordinary morning the city would ever know.
