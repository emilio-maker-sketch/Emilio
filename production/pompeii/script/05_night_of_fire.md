# Chapter Five — Night of Fire

[card: CHAPTER FIVE | Night of Fire]

[v: Vesuvius eruption 1779 painting ; Volaire Vesuvius]
[date: NIGHT, DAY ONE]
[mood: tense]
For about twelve hours, the eruption column stood above Vesuvius like a pillar holding up the sky. As long as it stayed upright, the danger to the towns around the mountain came from above: the falling pumice, the collapsing roofs, the choking ash. But an eruption column is a delicate thing. It stays aloft only as long as the volcano can keep heating the air around it, keeping the whole mass lighter than the atmosphere.

[v: Plinian eruption column ; Mount St Helens eruption 1980]
Sometime around midnight, the column began to falter. Perhaps the vent widened, or the gas in the magma began to run out. Whatever the cause, parts of the column became too heavy to rise. And when they could no longer rise, they fell.

[v: Pyroclastic flow ; Volcanic ash cloud]
[mood: dark]
What came down the slopes of Vesuvius was a pyroclastic current: a ground-hugging avalanche of scalding gas, ash and rock. Some of these currents are dense flows that pour down valleys like liquid. Others, called surges, are more dilute and turbulent, racing across the landscape at speeds of well over a hundred kilometres an hour. They can be hundreds of degrees hot. Nothing in their path survives.

[map: herculaneum]
The first of these currents swept down the western flank of the mountain, towards the sea. Directly in its path lay Herculaneum.

[v: Herculaneum ruins ; Herculaneum]
[mood: calm]
Herculaneum was a smaller town than Pompeii, home to perhaps four or five thousand people. It was a quieter, wealthier place, a resort of fine houses with terraces looking out over the bay. It had fewer shops and more villas, and its streets ran down towards the sea.

[v: Villa of the Papyri ; Herculaneum papyrus scroll]
Just outside the town stood one of the grandest country houses in Italy, a villa stretching for some two hundred and fifty metres along the coast. It was probably owned by the family of Julius Caesar's father-in-law, Lucius Calpurnius Piso. Its gardens were filled with bronze and marble statues, and its rooms held a library of papyrus scrolls. This building is known today as the Villa of the Papyri, and its library is the only one to have survived from the classical world.

[v: Herculaneum skeletons boat sheds ; Herculaneum ruins]
[mood: tense]
In the hours before the surge arrived, hundreds of the people of Herculaneum made their way down to the beach. Along the waterfront, beneath a great terrace, there was a row of vaulted stone chambers, used as boat sheds and storerooms. People crowded inside them, perhaps hoping for shelter from the falling ash, or waiting for boats from the fleet at Misenum that might carry them to safety.

[v: Herculaneum skeletons boat sheds]
For centuries, archaeologists believed that almost everyone in Herculaneum had escaped, because very few bodies had been found in the town itself. Then, in the nineteen-eighties, excavators reached the ancient shoreline. In the boat sheds, and on the beach in front of them, they found the remains of more than three hundred people.

[v: Herculaneum skeletons boat sheds ; Herculaneum]
[mood: dark]
They were men, women and children. Many were huddled together at the back of the vaulted chambers. Some held their belongings: coins, jewellery, keys, lamps. One woman wore gold rings and bracelets. A soldier lay on the beach, his sword still in its scabbard, his carpenter's tools on his back. Near the water lay an overturned boat.

[v: Pyroclastic flow ; Herculaneum skeletons boat sheds]
When the surge reached them, sometime after midnight, the temperature in the boat sheds may have risen to four hundred degrees or more in an instant. Death was almost immediate. Studies of the skeletons suggest that the heat was so intense that, in some victims, it vaporised soft tissue and caused the skull to fracture. In one recent study, scientists found that the brain of a young man killed nearby had been turned to glass by the sudden heat, then rapid cooling.

[v: Herculaneum ruins ; Villa of the Papyri]
The surge buried the town under a layer of hot ash. And it was only the first. Over the following hours, more currents poured down the mountain, one after another. In the end, Herculaneum lay beneath more than twenty metres of volcanic material, which hardened into a rock-like layer. The shoreline itself moved hundreds of metres out to sea.

[v: Roman bread carbonized Pompeii ; Pompeii carbonized food]
Because Herculaneum was buried by hot ash rather than falling pumice, it was preserved differently from Pompeii. Wood was carbonised rather than destroyed. Archaeologists have found wooden beds, cupboards, doors, and staircases, even food left on tables and loaves of bread still in the ovens. The upper floors of houses survive in places, and the town gives us a sense of what a Roman street looked like as a living, three-dimensional space.

[v: Herculaneum papyrus scroll ; Villa of the Papyri]
In the Villa of the Papyri, the heat turned the scrolls of the library into lumps of charcoal, rolled and fused so tightly that they looked like burnt logs. They would lie in the dark for more than sixteen centuries. And, as we will see, their story was far from over.

[mood: tense]
Meanwhile, in Pompeii, the pumice was still falling. The night was filled with the roar of the mountain, the crash of collapsing roofs, and the sound of stones raining down on the town. Those who had stayed behind waited for the dawn.

[v: Pompeii ruins panorama ; Pompeii street]
They did not know that the first surges were already sweeping down the slopes. The first currents did not reach Pompeii. They stopped short of the city walls. But each new current reached a little further than the one before.

[v: Vesuvius eruption painting ; Pierre-Jacques Volaire]
[mood: dark]
Dawn came, but in Pompeii, it brought no light. And at around half past six in the morning, the next current came down the mountain.
