# Chapter Four — The Admiral

[card: CHAPTER FOUR | The Admiral]

[v: Pliny the Elder ; Misenum Roman fleet]
[mood: calm]
Pliny the Elder's first instinct was the instinct of a scientist. Here was a natural phenomenon unlike anything he had ever seen, and he wanted to observe it at close quarters. He ordered a light, fast ship, a Liburnian, to be made ready, and he offered to take his nephew along.

[v: Pliny the Younger ; Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum]
The young Pliny declined. He had, he said, some studying to do. In fact, his uncle had set him a piece of writing to work on. Years later, he would admit, with a hint of embarrassment, that he had chosen his books over the greatest spectacle of his life.

[v: Roman trireme relief ; Misenum Roman fleet]
[mood: tense]
As Pliny the Elder was leaving the house, a message arrived. It came from a woman named Rectina, the wife of a man called Tascius, whose villa lay at the foot of the mountain. She was terrified by the danger threatening her, and her only escape was by sea. She begged him to rescue her.

[quote: What he began with a philosophical, he pursued with an heroical turn of mind. | Pliny the Younger, Letter 6.16 (tr. Melmoth)]
In that moment, everything changed. What had begun as a scientific expedition became a rescue mission. The admiral ordered the great warships of the fleet, the quadriremes, to be launched, and set out himself to bring help not only to Rectina, but to the many people living along that lovely stretch of coast.

[map: voyage]
The fleet sailed east across the bay, straight towards the danger, while everyone else was fleeing from it. According to his nephew, Pliny remained so calm that he dictated notes on every movement and shape of the eruption as he observed it, while a secretary wrote them down.

[v: Volcanic ash cloud ; Pompeii pumice lapilli]
As the ships drew closer to the shore, ash began to fall on the decks, hotter and thicker the nearer they came. Then came pieces of pumice, and blackened stones, cracked and burnt by fire. Suddenly the water became shallow. The shoreline was blocked by debris from the mountain, and the ships could not land.

[v: Roman quinquereme ; Bay of Naples]
For a moment, Pliny hesitated. His helmsman urged him to turn back. The admiral refused.

[quote: Fortune favours the brave. Head for Pomponianus. | Pliny the Elder, as reported by his nephew]
Fortune favours the brave, he said. Steer for Pomponianus.

[map: stabiae]
Pomponianus was a friend who lived at Stabiae, a few kilometres south of Pompeii, on the far side of the bay. With the wind behind them, the ships reached the shore there. Stabiae was not yet in immediate danger, but the danger was clearly coming. Pomponianus had already loaded his belongings onto boats, ready to escape as soon as the wind turned. But the same wind that had carried Pliny to Stabiae made it impossible to sail away.

[v: Villa San Marco Stabiae ; Stabiae villa fresco]
[mood: calm]
Pliny embraced his frightened friend and tried to comfort him. To show that there was nothing to fear, he asked to be taken to the baths. Afterwards, he dined cheerfully, or at least, his nephew writes, with every appearance of cheerfulness, which was just as brave.

[v: Vesuvius eruption 1779 painting ; Joseph Wright of Derby Vesuvius]
[mood: tense]
Night fell. On the slopes of Vesuvius, broad sheets of flame and tall columns of fire were blazing in several places, bright and vivid against the darkness. Pliny tried to reassure his companions, telling them these were only bonfires left by peasants who had fled their farms, or empty houses burning.

[v: Stabiae villa fresco ; Pompeii peristyle garden]
Then he went to bed, and, according to his nephew, fell into a genuine sleep. He was a large man, and his heavy breathing could be heard by those passing his door.

[v: Pompeii pumice lapilli ; Pumice]
But outside, the courtyard that led to his room was filling with ash and pumice. If he had stayed there any longer, he would have been trapped inside. The household woke him. He joined Pomponianus and the others, who had been awake all night, and together they debated what to do.

[v: Pompeii atrium impluvium ; Pompeii ruins panorama]
The buildings were shaking with violent tremors. They seemed to sway from side to side, as if they had been torn from their foundations. But outside, the falling stones, though light and porous, were a danger of their own. Weighing the two risks, they chose the open air.

[v: Pompeii pumice lapilli ; Volcanic ash cloud]
[date: MORNING, DAY TWO]
They tied pillows over their heads with strips of cloth, as protection against the falling stones, and set out. Elsewhere, it was now day. But around them, it was a night blacker and denser than any ordinary night, lit only by torches and lamps.

[v: Bay of Naples ; Roman quinquereme]
They went down to the shore to see whether the sea would let them escape. But the waves were still high and violent. Pliny lay down on a sheet spread on the sand, and asked again and again for cold water to drink.

[v: Pyroclastic flow ; Volcanic ash cloud]
[mood: dark]
Then flames, and a strong smell of sulphur, warning of fire to come, drove the others to flight. Pliny got to his feet, leaning on two slaves, and then suddenly collapsed. His nephew believed that the dense fumes had choked him, blocking his windpipe, which had always been weak and narrow and often inflamed.

[v: Pliny the Elder]
When daylight returned, two days after he had last been seen, his body was found on the beach, intact and uninjured, still dressed in the clothes he had been wearing. He looked, his nephew wrote, more like a man asleep than dead.

[v: Pliny the Elder ; Misenum Roman fleet]
Modern scientists have debated what killed him. He may have suffered a heart attack, or been overcome by gas, or by the hot, thin edge of one of the deadly surges that swept the bay that morning. What is certain is that the greatest naturalist of the Roman world died where he had chosen to be: watching nature at its most terrible, and trying to save the people caught in its path.

[v: Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum ; Pliny the Younger]
[mood: tense]
Back at Misenum, his nephew and his sister knew none of this. And they were about to face the eruption themselves.
