# Chapter Six — The Last Morning

By the morning of the second day, Pompeii was half buried. The pumice lay more than two and a half metres deep in the streets, reaching up to the windows of the ground floors. Roofs had collapsed across the city. Doorways were blocked. The town's gates had become low tunnels in a grey landscape of stone.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Pompeii body cast]
Those who were still alive had spent the night in darkness and terror. Now, as the fall of pumice began to ease, many of them decided it was time to go. They climbed out of their houses, onto the surface of the pumice, and began to make their way across the rooftops and along the half-buried streets towards the city gates.

[v: Pyroclastic flow ; Volcanic ash cloud]
[mood: dark]
They never made it. A little after half past six in the morning, a surge of hot gas and ash poured over the city walls and through the streets. It was moving perhaps a hundred kilometres an hour. It was hot enough to kill instantly. Within minutes, everyone left in Pompeii was dead.

[v: Garden of the Fugitives Pompeii]
In a vineyard near the southern edge of the city, now known as the Garden of the Fugitives, thirteen people were caught as they tried to escape towards the Nuceria gate. They were adults and children, perhaps two or three families travelling together, carrying their few possessions. They fell where they stood, on top of the pumice, in a line across the garden.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Pompeii body cast]
Other victims were found all across the city. A man sitting against a wall, his head in his hands. A group sheltering in a room, clinging to each other. A dog, still chained to its post, twisted in agony. People on the roads outside the gates, carrying bags of coins and jewellery. People who had stayed in their houses, and people who had almost reached the sea.

[v: Pompeii cast dog ; Pompeii body cast]
For many years, it was thought that most of these victims died by suffocating in the ash. More recent research suggests that, for many, death came even more quickly, from the sudden and extreme heat of the surge. Either way, it was over in moments.

[v: Pompeii ruins panorama ; Pompeii forum Vesuvius]
Further currents followed through the morning, each one burying the city deeper. The final layers of ash brought the total depth of debris in Pompeii to around six metres. The city vanished. Only the tops of the tallest buildings, and the upper parts of the great walls, still stood above the grey surface.

Thirty kilometres away, at Misenum, Pliny the Younger and his mother had spent the night in their house. The earthquakes had continued throughout the night, growing so violent that everything seemed not merely to shake, but to be turned upside down. His mother burst into his room. Together, they went out into the courtyard between the house and the sea.

[v: Pliny the Younger ; Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum]
And there, astonishingly, the seventeen-year-old sat down and asked for a book. He began to read the historian Livy, and even made notes, as if nothing were wrong. He would later wonder whether to call his behaviour courage or foolishness. A friend of his uncle, who had just arrived from Spain, found them sitting there and scolded them for their calm.

[date: 7 AM, DAY TWO]
[v: Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum ; Volcanic ash cloud]
It was now the first hour of the day, but the light was still dim and faint. The buildings around them were tottering, and they decided to leave the town. A panicked crowd followed them, pressing on behind.

[v: Bay of Naples ; Volcanic ash cloud]
Once outside the buildings, they stopped. The carts they had ordered were rolling in different directions, though the ground was flat, and they would not stay still even when wedged with stones. The sea seemed to be drawn back, sucked away by the earthquakes. The shoreline had moved outwards, and many sea creatures were left stranded on the dry sand.

[quote: A black and dreadful cloud, broken with rapid, zigzag flashes, revealed behind it variously shaped masses of flame. | Pliny the Younger, Letter 6.20 (tr. Melmoth, rev. Hutchinson)]
On the other side, a terrible black cloud, torn by sudden bursts of fire, split open to reveal long tongues of flame, like flashes of lightning, but larger. Soon afterwards, the cloud began to sink down to the earth and cover the sea. It had already hidden the island of Capri, and the headland of Misenum was lost from view.

[v: Volcanic ash cloud ; Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum]
His mother begged him to escape however he could. She was old and heavy, she said, and she would die happy if she knew she had not been the cause of his death. He refused to leave without her. He took her hand and made her walk faster. She went with difficulty, blaming herself for slowing him down.

[mood: dark]
Ash began to fall, though not yet heavily. He looked back. A dense black cloud was rolling up behind them, spreading over the ground like a flood. He suggested they leave the road while they could still see, so they would not be knocked down and trampled in the darkness by the crowd.

[quote: Not like that of a cloudy night, or when there is no moon, but of a room when it is shut up, and all the lights put out. | Pliny the Younger, Letter 6.20 (tr. Melmoth)]
They had barely sat down when darkness fell. It was not the darkness of a moonless or cloudy night, but the darkness of a sealed room when the lamp has been put out.

[v: Volcanic ash cloud ; Brullov Last Day of Pompeii]
You could hear the shrieks of women, the crying of children, and the shouts of men. Some were calling for their parents, others for their children or their husbands, trying to recognise them by their voices. Some lamented their own fate, others the fate of their families. Some prayed for death, out of fear of death.

[quote: The greater part imagined there were no gods left, and that the last and eternal night was come upon the world. | Pliny the Younger, Letter 6.20 (tr. Melmoth)]
Many raised their hands to the gods. But even more believed that there were no gods left anywhere, and that this was the last night of the world, the final, eternal darkness.

[v: Pompeii pumice lapilli ; Volcanic ash cloud]
A gleam of light returned, but it was not daylight. It was the glow of approaching fire. The fire stopped some distance away, and darkness came again, with ash falling thick and heavy. Every so often they had to stand up and shake it off, or they would have been buried and crushed by its weight.

[v: Pliny the Younger ; Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum]
Pliny admitted that he did not cry out or complain, even in that danger. But he said he drew some comfort from a grim thought: that he was dying together with the whole world, and the world together with him.

At last the darkness thinned into a kind of smoke or fog. Then real daylight returned. The sun even shone, though pale and yellow, as it does during an eclipse. Their eyes, still terrified, saw a world transformed. Everything was buried deep in ash, as if under a fall of snow.

[v: Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum]
They returned to Misenum and spent an anxious night, swinging between hope and fear. The earthquakes went on. They refused to leave until they had news of the admiral. It would come the next day: Pliny the Elder was dead.

[v: Vesuvius crater ; Vesuvius aerial]
[mood: dark]
By the evening of the second day, the eruption was over. In less than twenty-four hours, Vesuvius had thrown out something like four cubic kilometres of rock and ash. Pompeii, Herculaneum, Stabiae, Oplontis and countless farms and villas had been buried. The coastline had changed shape. The river Sarno had shifted its course.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Garden of the Fugitives Pompeii]
How many people died, we will never know exactly. More than eleven hundred bodies have been found in Pompeii so far, and over three hundred in Herculaneum, but a third of Pompeii remains unexcavated, and many victims must have died on the roads, on the beaches, and in the countryside. Most estimates suggest that several thousand people lost their lives. Many more lost their homes, their livelihoods, and their world.
