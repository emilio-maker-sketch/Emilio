# Chapter Three — The Seventh Hour

[card: CHAPTER THREE | The Seventh Hour]

[map: misenum]
[mood: calm]
Across the bay from Pompeii, about thirty kilometres to the west, lay the naval base of Misenum. Here, in a villa overlooking the water, lived one of the most remarkable men of the Roman world.

[v: Pliny the Elder]
His name was Gaius Plinius Secundus. We know him today as Pliny the Elder. He was fifty-six years old, a close friend of the late emperor Vespasian, and the commander of the Roman fleet stationed at Misenum. But he was also something rarer: a scholar of almost limitless curiosity. His great work, the Natural History, was an encyclopaedia of everything the Romans knew about the world, from the stars to the stones, from plants and animals to medicine and art.

[v: Pliny the Younger ; Pliny the Younger and his mother at Misenum]
Staying with him at Misenum were his widowed sister, Plinia, and her son, also called Gaius Plinius. He was seventeen years old, and the uncle had adopted him as his heir. History knows him as Pliny the Younger. And it is through his eyes that we see what happened next.

[v: Tacitus]
Some twenty-five years later, the historian Tacitus asked Pliny the Younger to describe how his uncle had died, so that he could include the account in his history of Rome. Pliny answered in two letters. Tacitus's account of the eruption is lost. But Pliny's letters survived, copied and recopied by hand through the centuries. They are the only eyewitness description of the eruption that has come down to us, and the first detailed description of a volcanic eruption in history.

At about the seventh hour of the day, a little after midday, Pliny's mother came to her brother and pointed out a cloud of unusual size and appearance. The admiral had been resting in the sun, then bathing, and had just eaten lunch. He called for his sandals and climbed to a spot where he could get a better view.

[quote: It shot up to a great height in the form of a very tall trunk, which spread itself out at the top into a sort of branches. | Pliny the Younger, Letter 6.16 (tr. Melmoth)]
From that distance, nobody could tell which mountain the cloud was coming from. Only later would they learn it was Vesuvius. Pliny the Younger compared its shape to an umbrella pine, the tall Mediterranean tree with a bare trunk and a flat, spreading crown. It rose high into the sky on a kind of trunk, he wrote, and then spread out into branches. Some of it was white, and some of it was dirty and spotted, as if it carried earth and ash.

[v: Plinian eruption column ; Pinatubo eruption 1991 ash column]
What he was describing, we now know, was an eruption column. Gas-rich magma, rising from deep beneath the volcano, had reached the surface and exploded, shattering into fragments of glass and pumice. The blast hurled this material upward at hundreds of metres per second. As it rose, it pulled in air, heated it, and the whole mass became buoyant, climbing into the stratosphere like smoke from a chimney. When it could rise no further, it spread out sideways, forming the canopy that reminded Pliny of a pine tree.

[v: Mount St Helens eruption 1980 ; Plinian eruption column]
In his honour, volcanologists today call this type of eruption a Plinian eruption. At its height, the column above Vesuvius probably reached more than thirty kilometres into the sky, nearly four times the height of Mount Everest. Every second, the volcano may have been throwing out over a hundred thousand tonnes of rock and ash.

High above the bay, the winds were blowing towards the south and east. And so the vast cloud did not drift over Misenum, or over Naples, or even over Herculaneum, which lay closest to the mountain. Instead, it drifted directly over Pompeii.

[v: Pompeii pumice lapilli ; Pumice]
In Pompeii, the sky darkened. Then the stones began to fall. They were pieces of pumice, a light, frothy volcanic rock full of gas bubbles, most of them no bigger than a walnut. At first, they were white. Later, as the eruption tapped deeper into the volcano, they turned grey. They fell like hail, rattling on the roof tiles and bouncing off the paving stones.

[v: Pompeii street ; Via dell'Abbondanza Pompeii]
The pumice was not, by itself, deadly. But it never stopped. It fell at a rate of perhaps fifteen centimetres an hour, hour after hour, through the afternoon and into the night. It filled the streets and the gardens, piled up in the courtyards, and blocked doorways. Mixed with it came larger rocks, torn from the walls of the volcano's throat, heavy enough to kill anyone they struck.

[v: Pompeii ruins panorama ; Pompeii atrium impluvium]
For the people of Pompeii, the choice was terrible. Should they stay indoors, sheltered from the falling stones, and wait for the storm to pass? Or should they risk the open roads, with cushions and pillows tied over their heads, and try to escape?

[v: Pompeii Porta Marina ; Pompeii walls tower]
Many chose to flee, and most of those who did, during these first hours, seem to have survived. They streamed out of the city gates towards Nuceria and the south, or down to the harbour in the hope of finding a boat. The sea, however, offered little help. The water was choked with floating pumice, and violent waves made it dangerous to launch.

[v: Pompeii House of the Faun ; Pompeii peristyle garden]
Others stayed behind. Some were too old, too sick, or too poor to leave. Some stayed to guard their houses and their valuables. Some, perhaps, simply could not believe that the danger was real. They barricaded themselves in their homes, in the darkness, listening to the stones drumming on the roof.

[v: Pompeii body cast ; Pompeii atrium impluvium]
[mood: dark]
As the evening wore on, the weight of pumice on the flat roofs became unbearable. Roof beams cracked. Ceilings gave way. People sheltering inside were crushed, or trapped in their rooms as the stones poured in through the gaps. By the end of the eruption, the pumice in Pompeii would lie nearly three metres deep.

[v: Herculaneum ruins ; Herculaneum]
Ten kilometres away, on the other side of the mountain, Herculaneum was spared the pumice. The wind carried the falling stones away from it, and only a light dusting of ash reached the town. Its people could see the immense cloud towering over the mountain. They could hear its roar. But for now, the sky above them was still relatively clear. Many of them fled. Others went down to the beach, where they waited for rescue that might come from across the water.

[v: Misenum Roman fleet ; Roman quinquereme]
[mood: rising]
And across the water, at Misenum, the admiral was already preparing to set sail.
