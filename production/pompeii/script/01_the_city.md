# Chapter One — The City

[card: CHAPTER ONE | The City]

[map: italy]
[mood: calm]
To understand what was lost, we first have to understand what Pompeii was. The city stood near the mouth of the river Sarno, on the southern edge of the Bay of Naples, about ten kilometres from the summit of Vesuvius. In the first century, this was one of the richest and most beautiful stretches of coastline in the entire Roman world.

[map: bay]
The bay was ringed with towns. Naples, the old Greek city, lay to the north. Herculaneum, a small and elegant seaside town, sat at the foot of the mountain on its western side. Further south were Stabiae, with its grand villas, and Oplontis, where some of the wealthiest families in Rome kept luxurious retreats overlooking the sea. At the northern tip of the bay, at Misenum, lay the headquarters of the most powerful navy in the Mediterranean.

[v: Pompeii walls tower ; Pompeii Porta Marina]
Pompeii itself was old, far older than Rome's control of it. It had been founded by a people called the Oscans, perhaps in the seventh or sixth century before Christ. Over the centuries, it had been influenced by Greek colonists, ruled by the Samnites, and finally drawn into the orbit of Rome.

[v: Sulla bust]
In the year eighty-nine before Christ, Pompeii made a terrible mistake. It joined a rebellion of Italian cities against Rome, and the Roman general Lucius Cornelius Sulla laid siege to its walls. The marks of his catapult stones can still be seen in the city's defences today. After the war, Sulla settled his veterans in the town, and Pompeii became a Roman colony, with a new Latin name: Colonia Cornelia Veneria Pompeianorum.

[v: Pompeii forum Vesuvius ; Pompeii Basilica ; Pompeii Temple of Apollo]
By the time of the eruption, the city had perhaps eleven or twelve thousand inhabitants, though some estimates run higher. At its heart was the forum, a long open square surrounded by temples, markets, and public buildings. Here stood the temple of Jupiter, with Vesuvius rising directly behind it, as if the god himself had chosen the mountain as his backdrop. Here, too, was the basilica, where merchants did business and magistrates heard legal cases.

[v: Pompeii street ; Via dell'Abbondanza Pompeii]
[mood: calm]
Pompeii's streets were paved with great blocks of volcanic stone, worn into deep ruts by the wheels of carts. Raised pavements kept pedestrians out of the mud and the sewage, and tall stepping stones allowed people to cross without getting their feet wet. Water flowed from public fountains at almost every street corner, fed by an aqueduct that brought fresh water down from the mountains.

[v: Pompeii thermopolium ; Pompeii bakery oven]
This was a city of commerce. Archaeologists have identified more than thirty bakeries, along with more than a hundred and fifty bars and eating houses, where the many people who had no kitchens of their own could buy a hot meal. Workshops processed wool, dyed cloth, and pressed olives. The city's fish sauce, called garum, was traded across the Mediterranean, and its wine was shipped as far away as Gaul and Spain.

[v: Roman wine amphorae Pompeii ; Roman amphora]
Much of that wealth came from the mountain itself. The volcanic soil on the slopes of Vesuvius was extraordinarily fertile, and vineyards covered its flanks. In a painting found in Pompeii, the god Bacchus stands beside a green mountain covered in vines. Most scholars believe it is Vesuvius, the only image of the volcano to survive from before the eruption. To the people of the bay, the mountain was not a threat. It was a source of plenty.

[v: Pompeii House of the Faun ; Pompeii Dancing Faun statue ; Alexander Mosaic]
The rich lived in style. The largest private home in the city, now known as the House of the Faun, covered an entire city block. Its floors were decorated with one of the most famous mosaics of the ancient world, a vast scene of Alexander the Great defeating the Persian king Darius, made from more than a million tiny pieces of stone.

[v: Villa of the Mysteries fresco ; Pompeii fresco]
The walls of Pompeii's houses were covered in painting. Bright reds, deep yellows and rich blacks framed scenes of gods, heroes, landscapes and gardens. Just outside the city walls, in a villa now known as the Villa of the Mysteries, an entire room was painted with life-size figures taking part in a mysterious religious ritual, its meaning still debated today.

[v: Pompeii fresco portrait Terentius Neo ; Pompeii fresco Sappho]
But Pompeii was not only a city of the rich. Its walls also preserve the voices of ordinary people. More than eleven thousand inscriptions have been recorded across the city: election slogans, advertisements, love notes, insults, and jokes. One graffito simply says, "I am amazed, O wall, that you have not fallen down under the weight of so much tedious writing."

[v: Pompeii graffiti ; Pompeii election inscription]
Others are more personal. Lovers declared their passions. Gladiators boasted of their victories. Someone noted the price of bread, someone else the date on which a slave was born. Together, they offer a picture of daily life in a Roman town more vivid than almost anything else that has come down to us from antiquity.

[v: Pompeii amphitheatre ; Pompeii Large Theatre]
For entertainment, there was the amphitheatre, the oldest surviving stone amphitheatre in the world, built around a hundred and fifty years before the Colosseum in Rome. It could hold perhaps twenty thousand spectators, more than the entire population of the town, drawing crowds from across the region. In the year fifty-nine, a brawl broke out there between the people of Pompeii and visitors from the neighbouring town of Nuceria. So many people were killed that the Roman Senate banned games in Pompeii for ten years.

[v: Pompeii Stabian Baths ; Pompeii peristyle garden]
There were theatres for comedy and music, public baths with hot and cold rooms, and gardens planted with fruit trees and flowers. Life in Pompeii was, by the standards of the ancient world, comfortable and full.

[v: Pompeii atrium impluvium ; Pompeii mosaic]
[mood: dark]
But beneath the vineyards and the painted walls, deep beneath the mountain, something was stirring. And the first warning had already come.
