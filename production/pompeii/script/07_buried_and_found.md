# Chapter Seven — Buried and Found

[card: CHAPTER SEVEN | Buried and Found]

[v: Emperor Titus bust]
[mood: calm]
News of the disaster reached Rome quickly. The new emperor, Titus, had been on the throne for only a few months. He sent senators to Campania to organise relief, and he used the property of victims who had died without heirs to help the survivors rebuild their lives. He visited the region himself. But there was no attempt to rebuild Pompeii or Herculaneum. The cities were simply too deeply buried.

[v: Pompeii ruins panorama ; Pompeii walls tower]
In the months after the eruption, some survivors and looters did return. They dug tunnels down through the ash to recover valuables, statues and building materials. Archaeologists have found their holes, punched through walls, and even graffiti left by these early visitors. But in time, even they stopped coming.

[v: Pompeii inscription ; Pompeii election inscription]
Where did the survivors go? In recent years, historians have tracked the distinctive family names of Pompeii and Herculaneum in inscriptions from nearby cities. They seem to have settled in Naples, in Cumae, in Puteoli, in Ostia, and in other towns around the region. They rebuilt their lives, married, worked, and were buried, far from the cities they had once called home.

[v: Vesuvius aerial ; Naples Vesuvius panorama]
[mood: calm]
Over the centuries, soil formed on top of the ash. Grass grew, then trees, then vineyards again. New farms and villages were built over the buried cities. The names of Pompeii and Herculaneum survived in old books, but their exact locations were forgotten. People called the area above Pompeii simply la Civita, the city, without knowing why.

[v: Vesuvius 1631 eruption ; Vesuvius eruption painting]
[date: AD 1631]
Vesuvius did not stay quiet. It erupted again and again over the following centuries, in the year four hundred and seventy-two, in the year five hundred and twelve, and many times after. In sixteen thirty-one, after a long period of calm, it erupted with tremendous violence, killing thousands of people in the towns around its base.

[v: Pompeii engraving 18th century ; Karl Weber Herculaneum plan]
In the late fifteen hundreds, an architect named Domenico Fontana dug a canal to bring water from the river Sarno. Its tunnel ran straight through the buried city of Pompeii. His workers cut through walls and even passed inscriptions. But nobody realised what they had found.

[v: Herculaneum ruins ; Herculaneum]
[date: AD 1709]
The first discoveries came by accident. In the early seventeen hundreds, a farmer digging a well near the town of Resina struck marble. It turned out he had dug straight down into the stage of the ancient theatre of Herculaneum. A prince serving in the Austrian army heard about it, bought the land, and had tunnels dug to extract statues for his collection.

[v: Charles III of Spain Naples]
In seventeen thirty-eight, the new king of Naples, Charles of Bourbon, ordered systematic excavations at Herculaneum. It was hard, dangerous work. The town lay beneath twenty metres of rock-hard volcanic material, and workers had to tunnel through it by candlelight, hauling out statues and frescoes without much concern for anything else.

[v: Pompeii engraving 18th century ; Pompeii excavation 19th century photograph]
[date: AD 1748]
Ten years later, in seventeen forty-eight, digging began at the site called la Civita. Because the ash there was much softer and shallower, the work was far easier. At first, the excavators thought they had found Stabiae. Only in seventeen sixty-three, when they uncovered an inscription naming the res publica Pompeianorum, the community of the Pompeians, did they realise that they had found Pompeii itself.

[v: Pompeii engraving 18th century ; Pompeii watercolour]
The discovery caused a sensation across Europe. Pompeii became an essential stop on the Grand Tour, the long journey through Italy that young aristocrats made to complete their education. Writers, artists and musicians came to see it: Goethe, Mozart, Dickens, Mark Twain. Painters filled their canvases with images of Roman life and of the city's final hours. Fashion, furniture and interior design across Europe began to imitate the colours and patterns of Pompeian walls.

[v: Pompeii excavation 1860s ; Giuseppe Fiorelli]
[date: AD 1863]
[mood: tense]
But the early excavations were often little more than treasure hunts. Then, in the eighteen sixties, a new director took charge: Giuseppe Fiorelli. He brought order to the site, dividing it into regions, blocks and houses, a numbering system still used today. And in eighteen sixty-three, he made a discovery that would change how the world saw Pompeii.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Pompeii body cast]
The excavators had noticed strange hollows in the hardened ash. Sometimes, bones lay at the bottom of them. Fiorelli realised what they were. The ash had hardened around the bodies of the victims before they decayed. Over time, the bodies had disappeared, leaving perfect moulds of their shapes in the rock.

[v: Pompeii plaster casts victims ; Pompeii body cast]
[mood: dark]
Fiorelli had liquid plaster poured into the hollows. When it set, the workers carefully chipped away the ash. What emerged were the figures of the people of Pompeii, captured in the moment of their death: their clothing, their sandals, the folds of their tunics, the expressions on their faces, the positions of their hands.

[v: Pompeii body cast ; Pompeii cast dog]
For the first time, the victims of Pompeii were no longer just skeletons. They were people. A man covering his mouth. A woman lying face down. A child curled against an adult. A dog, struggling against its chain. These casts, made with the victims' own bones inside them, are some of the most haunting objects ever found by archaeologists.

[v: Pompeii excavation 19th century photograph ; Pompeii ruins panorama]
[mood: calm]
The excavations went on through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. During the Second World War, Allied bombers mistakenly struck the site, damaging houses and the museum. And in nineteen forty-four, as the war raged across Italy, Vesuvius erupted once more, destroying dozens of Allied aircraft parked at an airfield near its base.

[v: Vesuvius 1944 eruption ; Vesuvius crater]
That eruption of nineteen forty-four was the last time Vesuvius erupted. Since then, it has been silent. But volcanologists agree on one thing. It is not a question of whether Vesuvius will erupt again. It is only a question of when.
