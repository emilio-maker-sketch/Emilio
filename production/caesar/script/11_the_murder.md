# CHAPTER ELEVEN: THE IDES OF MARCH

The conspirators gathered around his chair, as if to pay their respects. Tillius Cimber stepped forward with a petition. His brother was in exile, and he begged Caesar to recall him. The others joined in, pressing closer, taking Caesar's hands, kissing his chest and head. Caesar refused, and waved them away. Then Cimber grabbed his toga with both hands and pulled it down from his shoulders.

That was the signal. Caesar shouted: Why, this is violence! Casca, standing behind him, struck first. His dagger slipped. The blow landed near the neck, a shallow wound. Caesar turned and grabbed Casca's arm, and stabbed it with the only weapon he had, the sharp iron stylus he used for writing.

Casca shouted to his brother in Greek for help. And then they all closed in. Daggers came from every direction. In the confusion, the conspirators wounded one another. Cassius struck at his face. Brutus was wounded in the hand. The senators who were not in the plot sat frozen in horror, unable to move, unable to call out.

Caesar fought back, Plutarch says, turning this way and that like a wild animal. But when he saw Brutus coming at him with a dagger, he stopped resisting. He pulled his toga up over his head. With his left hand, Suetonius adds, he drew the folds down over his legs, so that he would fall decently, with his body covered.

He was stabbed twenty-three times. He fell at the foot of the statue of Pompey, which was splashed with his blood, so that, as Plutarch writes, it seemed that Pompey himself was presiding over the vengeance taken on his enemy.

What were his last words? Suetonius says that he said nothing at all, but only groaned at the first blow. Though, he adds, some writers claim that when he saw Marcus Brutus coming at him, he said in Greek: kai su, teknon? You too, child? Et tu, Brute? is not in any ancient source. It comes from the Elizabethan stage, and Shakespeare made it immortal.

Later, a doctor named Antistius examined the body. Of the twenty-three wounds, he reported, only one was fatal: the second, in the chest. Another early writer, Nicolaus of Damascus, says there were thirty-five. Whatever the number, the man who had survived Gaul, the civil war, Alexandria and Munda was dead on the floor of a hall built by the rival he had destroyed.

The whole attack may have lasted no more than a minute or two. The man who struck first was Casca, a senator whose name is now remembered for almost nothing else. Most of the conspirators struck only once, if at all. Some of the blows were symbolic. The point was that they had all taken part, so that none of them could later claim to be innocent.
