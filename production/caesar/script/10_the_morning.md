# CHAPTER TEN: THE MORNING OF THE IDES

That night, Caesar's wife Calpurnia dreamed of disaster. In one version, she held his murdered body in her arms. In another, the ornamental gable that the Senate had voted to decorate his house had collapsed. She woke in terror and begged him not to go out that day. Caesar, who had never been superstitious, was shaken. He was also feeling unwell. He decided to send Mark Antony to dismiss the Senate.

The priests had made sacrifices that morning, and the signs were bad. If Caesar stayed home, the plot was finished, and probably the conspirators too. So they sent the one man he trusted completely. Decimus Brutus came to the house, and mocked the omens gently. Was Caesar really going to make the Senate wait because his wife had had a bad dream? The senators would think him a tyrant, or a fool. At least come and dismiss them in person.

It was the late morning by the time Caesar left the house. He was carried through the streets in a litter, and as always people pressed forward with petitions. One of them was a Greek teacher of rhetoric named Artemidorus of Cnidos, who knew some of the conspirators. He pushed his way through and handed Caesar a small scroll, urging him to read it at once, alone, because it concerned him. Caesar held it in his hand. He never read it.

Inside the portico of Pompey, the conspirators waited, trying to look normal. Brutus was presiding as praetor, hearing legal cases. One of the senators, Popilius Laenas, came up to Brutus and Cassius and whispered that he hoped their business would succeed, and that they should hurry, because people were talking. They went cold. When Caesar arrived, Popilius went straight to him and spoke into his ear for a long time. The conspirators watched, ready, some say, to kill themselves if he revealed the plot. Then Caesar nodded, and Popilius kissed his hand and stepped back.

Outside the hall, the sacrifices were performed again. They were bad again. Caesar went in anyway. As he passed Spurinna, the soothsayer, he called out to him cheerfully that the Ides of March had come, and nothing had happened. Spurinna answered: yes, they have come. But they have not yet gone.

Outside, at the doors, Trebonius, or according to another account Decimus, drew Mark Antony aside and kept him talking. Antony was strong, loyal and a consul. He was the one man who might have fought back. He stayed outside.
