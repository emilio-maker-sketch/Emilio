# CHAPTER THIRTEEN: THE WILL AND THE FUNERAL

Then Caesar's will was opened and read aloud, at Antony's house. It changed everything. To every Roman citizen in the city, Caesar left three hundred sesterces, months of wages for a labourer. He left his gardens across the Tiber to the people of Rome as a public park. And as his principal heir he named his great-nephew, an eighteen-year-old most Romans had never heard of, Gaius Octavius. In a final clause, he adopted him as his son.

There was worse for the assassins. Among the heirs named in case the first ones failed was Decimus Brutus. The man who had brought Caesar to the Senate that morning had been named in his will. When the people heard, the mood in the city turned.

The funeral was held a few days later, probably on the twentieth of March. A gilded shrine, modelled on the Temple of Venus Genetrix, was set up on the speaker's platform in the Forum. Inside it lay Caesar's body on an ivory couch draped in purple and gold. At its head hung the toga he had been wearing when he was killed.

What Mark Antony actually said that day is uncertain. Suetonius says he made only a brief speech, and had a herald read out the honours the Senate had voted Caesar, and the oath by which they had sworn to protect him. The later historian Appian gives him a long, passionate oration, and says that he held up the bloodstained, slashed toga on the point of a spear. The great speech that begins, Friends, Romans, countrymen, is Shakespeare's.

Appian also describes something extraordinary. Above the body, a wax image of Caesar was raised, showing all twenty-three wounds, and turned slowly on a mechanism so that the whole crowd could see it. If the detail is true, it was one of the most effective pieces of political theatre in history.

The crowd exploded. Instead of carrying the body to the pyre prepared on the Field of Mars, they built one right there in the Forum, from benches, tables, the judges' platforms, anything that would burn. Veterans threw their weapons into the flames. Musicians and actors tore off their costumes and threw them in. Women threw in their jewellery. Caesar was cremated in the heart of Rome.

Then the mob went looking for the assassins. They carried burning brands to the houses of Brutus and Cassius, and were driven back. In the chaos they caught a man called Helvius Cinna, a poet and a friend of Caesar, and mistook him for Cornelius Cinna, one of Caesar's enemies who had praised the murder. They tore him apart in the street. Within weeks, Brutus and Cassius had left Rome. They would never return.
