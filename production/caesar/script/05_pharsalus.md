# CHAPTER FIVE: PHARSALUS

The war that followed spread across the whole Roman world. Caesar secured Spain first. Then, in the winter of 49 BC, he crossed the Adriatic in pursuit of Pompey, slipping past the enemy fleet with only part of his army. At Dyrrhachium, on the coast of what is now Albania, Pompey beat him badly. Caesar is said to have remarked that the war would have been lost that day if the other side had had a commander who knew how to win.

He retreated inland to Thessaly, in northern Greece, and Pompey followed him. On the ninth of August, 48 BC, the two armies met on the plain of Pharsalus. Pompey had perhaps twice as many men, and far more cavalry. The senators in his camp were so sure of victory that they had already started arguing over who would get Caesar's offices and houses.

Caesar had expected Pompey's cavalry to sweep round his flank. Behind his lines he hid a fourth line of veteran infantry. When the horsemen charged, these men rose up and drove their spears at the riders, and the cavalry broke and fled. Pompey's army collapsed. Pompey himself rode back to his camp, sat in his tent in silence, and then fled.

Walking across the battlefield afterwards, among thousands of dead Romans, Caesar is reported to have said, they wanted this. After all I had done, I, Gaius Caesar, would have been condemned if I had not turned to my army for help. Among the enemy soldiers who surrendered that day and were pardoned was a young officer named Marcus Junius Brutus.

Pompey's army had been made up of Romans, and so had Caesar's. This was the cost of civil war. According to Caesar's own account, about fifteen thousand of Pompey's men died and twenty-four thousand surrendered. He lost, he claims, only two hundred men, though thirty of them were centurions. Historians treat these figures with suspicion. Caesar was the only one counting.

Pompey fled east, and then to Egypt, where he hoped for protection from the young king Ptolemy the Thirteenth, whose father he had once helped. The boy king's advisers did their sums. On the twenty-eighth of September, as Pompey stepped from a small boat onto the beach at Pelusium, in full view of his wife on the ship behind him, he was stabbed to death by a Roman officer who had once served under him.

A few days later, Caesar arrived at Alexandria. The Egyptians brought him a gift: Pompey's head, and his signet ring, engraved with a lion holding a sword. According to Plutarch, Caesar turned away from the head in disgust and wept when he took the ring. The man who had been his ally, his son-in-law, and finally his enemy, was dead. Caesar was now alone at the top.
