# CHAPTER FOUR: THE RUBICON

By 50 BC, Caesar's enemies in the Senate had a plan. His command in Gaul gave him legal immunity. The moment he gave it up and returned to Rome as a private citizen, they would prosecute him for the illegal acts of his consulship and finish his career. Caesar's answer was to stand for a second consulship without coming home, moving straight from one office to the next. His enemies, led by the stern Marcus Porcius Cato, refused.

And Pompey, who had once been Caesar's partner and son-in-law, now sided with the Senate. On the first of January, 49 BC, the Senate ordered Caesar to lay down his command. Two tribunes who supported him, one of them a young officer named Mark Antony, vetoed the decree. They were threatened, and fled the city disguised as slaves. The Senate passed its emergency decree, calling on the magistrates to defend the state. It was a declaration of war.

Caesar was at Ravenna, near the border of his province, with a single legion. The border was a small river called the Rubicon. Under Roman law, a general who led his army across it into Italy without permission became an enemy of the state. On the night of the tenth of January, he quietly left a dinner party, took a carriage pulled by mules from a nearby bakery, got lost in the dark, and reached the river at dawn.

What happened at the riverbank is told in different ways. Suetonius says he hesitated, and then said, in Latin, the die is cast. Plutarch says he spoke the words in Greek, quoting a line from the playwright Menander, and that he spent a long time going over the consequences before he crossed. Every version agrees on one thing. He crossed.

Nobody today is certain which river was the Rubicon. Courses have shifted over two thousand years. The stream that now bears the name was officially designated only in the twentieth century. But the phrase crossing the Rubicon has meant the point of no return ever since.

Italy fell almost without a fight. Town after town opened its gates. Pompey, who had boasted that he only had to stamp his foot for armies to rise out of the ground, found that he had no army ready. He abandoned Rome and retreated south to Brundisium, and in March he sailed across the Adriatic to Greece, taking most of the Senate with him.

Caesar surprised everyone. Sulla had answered victory with death lists. Caesar pardoned the enemy officers he captured and let them go, even when he knew they would fight against him again. He called it his clemency. His enemies understood the insult hidden inside it. To be pardoned by Caesar was to admit that your life was his to give.
