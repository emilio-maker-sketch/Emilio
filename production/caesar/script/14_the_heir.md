# CHAPTER FOURTEEN: THE HEIR

When Caesar died, Gaius Octavius was across the Adriatic, at Apollonia, in what is now Albania, studying and training with the legions gathering for the Parthian war. He was eighteen, slight, often ill. His family begged him not to accept the inheritance. It was far too dangerous. He accepted it anyway, and took his adoptive father's name. From now on, he called himself Gaius Julius Caesar.

Mark Antony did not take him seriously. Cicero later quoted him mocking Octavian as a boy who owed everything to a name. But the name was everything. Caesar's veterans flocked to him. He paid the three hundred sesterces of Caesar's will to the people out of his own pocket, and when Antony refused to hand over the money, he sold his own property to do it.

In July of 44 BC, during games in Caesar's honour, a bright comet appeared in the sky and shone for seven days. Octavian himself, as Pliny the Elder records, said the people believed it was Caesar's soul being received among the immortal gods. He had a star placed on the head of Caesar's statues. Caesar was on his way to becoming a god, and his heir to being the son of a god.

The next eighteen months were a blur of shifting alliances. Octavian first sided with the Senate against Antony. At the battle of Mutina, in April 43 BC, both consuls of the year were killed, and Octavian was left in command of their armies. He marched on Rome and made himself consul at nineteen. Then, to the Senate's horror, he made a deal with Antony and Lepidus.

The three men met on a small river island near Bononia, modern Bologna, and divided the Roman world between them. Modern historians call it the Second Triumvirate. Unlike the first, it was made legal, for five years. And like Sulla, they published lists. According to Appian, some three hundred senators and two thousand wealthy equestrians were proscribed, their property seized, their heads brought in for a reward.

The most famous name on the lists was Cicero's. Antony had never forgiven the series of brilliant speeches Cicero had delivered against him, the Philippics. On the seventh of December, 43 BC, soldiers caught the old orator at his villa near Formiae. He leaned out of his litter and offered them his neck. His head and his hands, the hands that had written the Philippics, were nailed up on the speaker's platform in the Forum.

The assassins were already being hunted. Trebonius was the first to die, killed at Smyrna in early 43 by one of Caesar's partisans. Decimus Brutus, the man who had brought Caesar to the Senate, was abandoned by his army, captured by a Gallic chieftain, and executed on Antony's orders. And in January 42 BC, the Senate formally declared Julius Caesar a god: Divus Iulius, the deified Julius.
