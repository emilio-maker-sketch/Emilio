# CHAPTER TWELVE: LIBERTY

Brutus turned to the senators to make a speech. He wanted to explain what they had done and why. But there was no one left to hear it. The senators who were not in the plot had fled the hall in panic, scrambling over the benches, pushing through the doors, spreading the news across the city as they ran. Rome did not hear a speech about liberty. It heard screaming.

The assassins marched out together through the streets toward the Capitol, holding up their bloody daggers and calling out that they had killed a king and a tyrant. One of them carried a felt cap on the end of a spear, the cap given to freed slaves, the symbol of liberty. They were joined by Decimus's gladiators. But the people did not cheer. They shut their doors.

For hours, Caesar's body lay alone in the hall. Finally, three slaves came, put him in a litter, and carried him home through the streets to the house on the Sacred Way. The curtains of the litter were drawn back, Suetonius says, and one arm hung down over the side.

Mark Antony, terrified that he was next, had fled home disguised as a slave and barricaded himself in. But the conspirators had made two mistakes. They had not killed him. And they had no plan for what came next. They had assumed that, with the tyrant gone, the Republic would simply restart itself. Instead, Antony went to Calpurnia that night and took charge of Caesar's papers and his money. Caesar's deputy Lepidus brought troops into the Forum.

In the meantime, Brutus spoke to the people from the Capitol, and then again in the Forum. The crowd listened in silence. They did not attack him, but they did not join him either. Many of them were Caesar's veterans, waiting in the city for the land they had been promised. They wanted to know whether the assassins would honour Caesar's grants, or take them away.

On the seventeenth of March, two days after the murder, the Senate met in the Temple of the Earth goddess, Tellus. It was a strange, tense compromise. On Cicero's proposal, the assassins were granted an amnesty. But Caesar was not declared a tyrant, and all his acts and appointments were confirmed. That suited almost everyone, because so many senators owed their positions to him. It also meant that the men who had killed a tyrant now had to live under the tyrant's laws.

Cicero, who had not been told about the plot, was overjoyed at the murder. But within weeks he saw what had gone wrong. In a letter to his friend Atticus, he wrote that the assassins had acted with the courage of men, but the judgment of boys. They had cut down the tree, he complained, but left the roots.
