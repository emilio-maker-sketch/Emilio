# CHAPTER SIXTEEN: THE SON OF A GOD

The alliance of the victors did not last. Lepidus was pushed aside. Antony took the East, and Cleopatra, and Octavian took the West. In 31 BC their fleets met at Actium, off the coast of Greece. Octavian won. A year later, in Alexandria, Antony and Cleopatra took their own lives. Caesarion, the boy who may have been Caesar's son, was hunted down and killed. One of Octavian's advisers is said to have told him that too many Caesars was not a good thing.

In 27 BC, Octavian made a grand show of handing power back to the Senate and the people. The grateful Senate gave him a new name: Augustus, the revered one. He kept the consuls, the Senate, the elections and the laws. He never called himself king, and never dictator. He was simply princeps, the first citizen. Behind the old forms, one man ruled Rome until his death, forty years later. Every ruler after him did the same. The Republic was over.

So who won? The assassins wanted to save the Republic. They killed the man they thought was a tyrant, and within twenty years the Republic had been replaced by an emperor more powerful than Caesar had ever been. Caesar's heir carried his name, completed his building projects, and worshipped him as a god. The daggers of the Ides of March did not stop the end of the Republic. In many ways, they finished it.

And yet the idea the conspirators died for did not die with them. Two thousand years later, the founders of new republics read Cicero and Plutarch. They argued about Caesar and about Brutus. Was he a tyrant? Was killing him murder, or a duty? Those questions have never quite been settled.

Caesar himself left no account of his own aims in those final months. His enemies said he wanted a crown. His friends said he wanted order. The truth may be that he did not think the Republic could be saved in its old form, and he did not much care. Suetonius quotes a hostile writer who claimed that Caesar called the Republic nothing, a mere name without body or form. If he really said it, he was half right. The forms survived him for centuries. The thing itself did not.

Some things we can say with confidence. The murder took place not on the Capitol, as Shakespeare has it, but in the Curia of Pompey. Caesar's last words were not et tu, Brute. Spurinna's warning was real enough to be remembered, but beware the Ides of March is a line from a play. And Caesar was killed not by a crowd of strangers, but by men he had pardoned, promoted and, in one case, named in his will.

His name outlived everyone. Caesar became the title of the Roman emperors, and then of the German Kaiser and the Russian Tsar. His month is still July. His calendar, with one correction, still divides our year.

And in the centre of modern Rome, in a sunken square called Largo di Torre Argentina, among the ruins of four Republican temples, you can still see part of the base of the hall where it happened. Cats sleep on the old stones. And in the Forum, at the altar built where his body burned, people still leave flowers every year, on the fifteenth of March.
