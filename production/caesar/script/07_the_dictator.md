# CHAPTER SEVEN: THE DICTATOR

Caesar now held more power than any Roman before him. He was named dictator, first for ten years, and then, in early 44 BC, for life: dictator perpetuo. He was consul as well, whenever he chose to be. Magistrates were elected, but they were the men he recommended. The Senate still met, but the decisions were made in his house.

And he used it. In just a few years he reformed the calendar, which had drifted by about three months out of step with the seasons. To fix it, the year 46 BC was stretched to four hundred and forty-five days. From the first of January, 45 BC, Rome used a new calendar of three hundred and sixty-five days, with a leap day every four years. With one later adjustment, it is the calendar we still use.

He enlarged the Senate to about nine hundred members, filling it with his own supporters, including provincials and soldiers' sons. He settled tens of thousands of veterans and poor citizens in new colonies abroad, and refounded Carthage and Corinth, cities Rome had destroyed a century before. He planned public libraries, a new theatre, a vast temple to Mars, the draining of marshes, a canal through the Isthmus of Corinth.

His new Forum rose beside the old one, dominated by a temple to Venus Genetrix, Venus the Mother, the goddess from whom his family claimed descent. Inside, next to the statue of the goddess, he placed a gilded statue of Cleopatra. The queen herself was living in his villa across the Tiber.

The honours piled up. His statue was set up on the Capitol beside those of the ancient kings. The month of his birth, Quintilis, was renamed Julius, July. His face appeared on Roman coins, the first living Roman ever given that honour. He was allowed to wear the purple robe and laurel wreath of a triumphing general whenever he liked. Suetonius notes, a little unkindly, that he was pleased by the wreath, because it hid his bald patch.

Then came the moments that his enemies never forgot. When a delegation of senators came to him at the Temple of Venus Genetrix to report the latest honours voted to him, he did not stand up to greet them. To the Senate it was an insult. Some sources say he was unwell. Others say he was about to rise when a friend told him to remember that he was Caesar.

At the festival of the Lupercalia, on the fifteenth of February, 44 BC, the consul Mark Antony ran up to him in the Forum in front of a huge crowd and offered him a royal diadem. Caesar refused it. Antony offered it again. Caesar refused again, and the crowd cheered. He ordered it to be recorded that he had refused the crown. Was it a test? A piece of theatre? A trap? Nobody knows. But in a city whose founding oath was never again a king, the question had been asked.
