# CHAPTER SIX: THE QUEEN AND THE WARS

Egypt was in the middle of its own civil war. The boy king Ptolemy shared his throne with his elder sister, Cleopatra the Seventh, twenty-one years old, clever, ruthless, and the first of her Greek-speaking dynasty said to have learned the Egyptian language. Her brother's advisers had driven her out of Alexandria. Caesar, who wanted Egypt's money, announced that he would settle the dispute.

Cleopatra needed to reach him without being caught by her brother's men. According to Plutarch, a single friend rowed her across the harbour at dusk, tied her inside a bedding sack, and carried her into the palace on his shoulder. The famous carpet comes from later retellings. Either way, Caesar was captivated, and within days he had taken her side.

The Alexandrians rose against him. For months Caesar was besieged in the palace quarter with only a few thousand men. During the fighting, fire spread from the burning ships in the harbour, and some ancient writers say that part of the great Library of Alexandria went up in flames. How much was lost is still debated. At one point Caesar had to jump into the harbour and swim for his life, holding his papers above the water.

In the spring of 47 BC, reinforcements arrived. The young king Ptolemy drowned in the Nile while fleeing the battle, and Cleopatra was placed on the throne with a younger brother. Caesar stayed on in Egypt for weeks, and the sources say he sailed with the queen up the Nile. That summer, Cleopatra gave birth to a son. She called him Caesarion, little Caesar. Whether he was really Caesar's child was argued over then and is argued over still.

Then Caesar went back to war. In Asia Minor, King Pharnaces of Pontus had taken advantage of the chaos. Caesar met him at Zela and destroyed his army in about four hours. He reported the campaign to Rome in three words. Veni, vidi, vici. I came, I saw, I conquered.

In Africa the next year, at Thapsus, he destroyed the last great army of the Senate's party. Cato, rather than accept Caesar's pardon, killed himself at Utica. When Caesar heard, he said he begrudged Cato his death, because Cato had begrudged him the chance to save his life. Back in Rome, Caesar celebrated four triumphs in a single month: over Gaul, Egypt, Pontus and Africa. Vercingetorix was led in chains through the streets and then put to death.

There was one more war. Pompey's sons raised a new army in Spain. At Munda, in March 45 BC, the battle went so badly for Caesar that he plunged into the front line himself. He said afterwards that he had often fought for victory, but this was the first time he had fought for his life. He won. The civil war was over.
