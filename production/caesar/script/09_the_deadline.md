# CHAPTER NINE: THE DEADLINE

The conspirators had a deadline. On the eighteenth of March, Caesar was due to leave Rome for the East, to fight a war against Dacia and then against Parthia that might last three years. Sixteen legions were being prepared. Once he was with his army, surrounded by soldiers who worshipped him, no one would be able to touch him. If they were going to act, it had to be before he left.

They considered the options. Attack him on the Sacred Way as he walked home? At the elections, as he stood on the bridge where voters crossed? At the gladiator games? In the end they chose a meeting of the Senate. Only senators could attend, so his friends and bodyguards would have to stay outside. And the conspirators could carry daggers hidden in their togas without arousing suspicion. If anything went wrong, Decimus had a troupe of gladiators at the theatre nearby.

The Senate was due to meet on the Ides of March, the fifteenth. Its usual home, the old Senate house in the Forum, had burned down eight years before, and the new one Caesar was building was not finished. So this meeting would be held in a hall attached to the great complex built by his dead rival: the Curia of Pompey, at the end of a long garden portico behind Pompey's theatre. A statue of Pompey stood inside.

Did Caesar suspect? He had been warned many times. He had dismissed his Spanish bodyguard. When friends urged him to protect himself, he is said to have answered that it was better to die once than to be always afraid of dying. He also argued that his survival was in the Republic's interest, more than his own. If anything happened to him, he said, Rome would be plunged into civil wars far worse than before.

The conspirators were not the only ones under strain. Brutus could hardly sleep. His wife, Porcia, the daughter of Cato, guessed that something was wrong. According to Plutarch, to prove that she could keep a secret, she cut deep into her own thigh with a small knife, hid the wound for a day, and only then showed him. Brutus told her everything.

There were omens too, or at least the ancient writers later remembered them. A soothsayer named Spurinna, reading the entrails of a sacrifice, warned Caesar of great danger that would come no later than the Ides of March. The horses that Caesar had released after crossing the Rubicon were said to be refusing to eat, and weeping. And on the day before the Ides, a small bird flew into the Curia of Pompey carrying a sprig of laurel, chased by other birds, who tore it to pieces.

On the evening of the fourteenth of March, Caesar dined at the house of his deputy, Marcus Lepidus. The talk turned to death, and someone asked what kind of death was the best. Before anyone else could answer, Caesar said: a sudden one.
