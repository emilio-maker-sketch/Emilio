# CHAPTER FIFTEEN: PHILIPPI

Brutus and Cassius had gone east, to the provinces of Greece and Asia, where they raised armies of their own, some eighty thousand men, and squeezed the cities of the region for money. And Brutus did something that no Roman tyrant-killer had done before. He struck a coin. On one side, his own portrait. On the other, a cap of liberty between two daggers, and two words: EID MAR. The Ides of March.

According to Plutarch, one night in his tent, as Brutus sat working by lamplight, a monstrous figure appeared beside him. Brutus asked who it was. The figure answered: I am your evil spirit, Brutus. You will see me at Philippi. Plutarch tells it as a story, and the modern reader can take it as one. But it shows how the ancient writers saw Brutus at the end: haunted.

In October 42 BC, the armies met at Philippi, in northern Greece. Antony and Octavian had about nineteen legions. Brutus and Cassius had a similar number. In the first battle, Brutus broke Octavian's wing and stormed his camp. Octavian, who was ill, was not there. But on the other wing, Antony crushed Cassius.

From his hill, Cassius could not see through the dust that Brutus had won. He sent an officer to find out. When he saw horsemen surround the man, he thought he had been captured. He went into his tent with his freedman Pindarus and ordered him to kill him. Plutarch says Cassius died by the same dagger he had used against Caesar.

Three weeks later, in the second battle, Brutus's army broke. That night, with a few friends, he killed himself. Plutarch says he fell on his sword, which was held by a friend. Antony, finding the body, covered it with his own purple cloak.

Before he died, according to Plutarch, Brutus said that he was happier than the victors. He was leaving behind, he said, a reputation for virtue that they would never have, for all their armies and their money. Whether he believed it, no one can say. When the body was found, Octavian ordered that the head be cut off and sent to Rome, to be thrown at the feet of Caesar's statue. It never arrived. According to Cassius Dio, it was lost in a storm at sea.

With Philippi, the last army of the Republic was destroyed. Suetonius says that hardly any of the assassins outlived Caesar by more than three years, and that none of them died a natural death. Some died in battle, some at sea, some were executed. Some, like Cassius, turned the same daggers on themselves. It is an exaggeration, but not by much.
