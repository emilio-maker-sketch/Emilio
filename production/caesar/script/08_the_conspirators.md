# CHAPTER EIGHT: THE CONSPIRATORS

Rumours spread. People whispered that Caesar wanted to be king, that he planned to move the capital to Alexandria, that a law was being drafted to let him marry as many wives as he wished. Most of it was gossip. But one rumour, reported by Suetonius, was more specific. An ancient prophecy in the Sibylline Books was said to declare that the Parthians could only be defeated by a king. And Caesar was about to march against the Parthians.

Late at night, statues of the ancient Brutus, the man who had driven out the last king, were found covered with messages. Would that you were alive. Your descendants are unworthy of you. And on the official seat of his descendant, Marcus Junius Brutus, now one of Rome's chief magistrates, someone wrote: Brutus, you are asleep. You are not really Brutus.

Brutus was forty-one, serious, bookish, admired for his integrity. He had fought against Caesar at Pharsalus and been pardoned. Since then Caesar had promoted him, and planned to make him consul. His mother, Servilia, had for years been Caesar's lover, which led to a rumour that Brutus was Caesar's son. It is almost certainly untrue. Caesar would have been about fifteen when Brutus was born.

The man who recruited him was his brother-in-law, Gaius Cassius Longinus. Cassius was a hard, proud soldier who had saved what was left of Crassus's army after Carrhae, and who had also been pardoned. He had a temper, and a grudge. Plutarch says Caesar distrusted him, and once remarked, of Cassius and Brutus, that he did not fear the fat, long-haired men. It was the pale, thin ones he worried about.

The third key man is less famous but may have been the most important of all: Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus, a distant cousin of Marcus. He had been one of Caesar's best generals in Gaul. Caesar trusted him completely, named him governor of a province, and, as the conspirators did not know, had listed him in his will. Other conspirators were Gaius Trebonius, another of Caesar's old officers, Publius Servilius Casca and his brother, and Lucius Tillius Cimber.

In the end there were more than sixty of them. Some were old Pompeians nursing their pride. Some were Caesar's own officers who felt cheated of the rewards they expected. Some, like Brutus, truly believed they were saving the Republic from a tyrant. Remarkably, the plot never leaked. Plutarch says no one swore an oath, and no one betrayed it.

They deliberately left out the most famous orator in Rome, Marcus Tullius Cicero. He shared their views, but they doubted his nerve. And they argued about Mark Antony, Caesar's loyal deputy and fellow consul. Cassius wanted to kill him too. Brutus refused. This was to be an act of justice against a tyrant, he said, not a massacre. It was a decision that would cost them everything.
