# CHAPTER ONE: THE REPUBLIC

To understand why Caesar died, you have to understand what the men who killed him believed they were defending. Rome had once been ruled by kings. According to Roman tradition, the last of them, Tarquin the Proud, was driven out in 509 BC by a nobleman named Lucius Junius Brutus. From then on, the Romans swore, no one would ever again be king in Rome.

In place of a king they built a republic, from the Latin res publica, the public thing. Every year the people elected two consuls to lead the state and command its armies. Each held power for just twelve months, and each could block the other. Below them came a ladder of other offices, all elected, all held for a single year.

At the centre stood the Senate, some three hundred former magistrates who served for life. The Senate had no formal power to make laws. But its advice was rarely ignored, and its members, a few hundred families who intermarried, competed and remembered every insult for generations, ran Rome.

The system was built for a city state. But by the first century BC, Rome ruled the Mediterranean, from Spain to Asia Minor. Its generals commanded armies far from home for years at a time. Its governors returned from the provinces with fortunes. And the soldiers who won its wars came to look to their generals, not to the Senate, for land and reward.

The century before Caesar was soaked in blood. In 133 BC, a reforming tribune named Tiberius Gracchus was clubbed to death by senators in the street. Twelve years later, his brother Gaius died in another outbreak of violence. Then the generals took over. Gaius Marius held the consulship seven times. His rival Sulla did something no Roman had ever done. He marched his army on Rome itself.

Behind the violence was a simple problem. Rome's wars had flooded Italy with slaves and with wealth, and the great landowners bought up the small farms that had once supplied the legions. Gracchus had tried to give land back to the poor. The senators who killed him saw it as a bid for kingship. That word, king, would be thrown at every popular politician for the next hundred years.

Sulla revived the dictatorship, an emergency office traditionally limited to six months, took it with no time limit at all, and used it to publish lists of his enemies. Anyone named could be killed by anyone, and the killer was rewarded with part of the victim's property. These were the proscriptions. Thousands died. Rome had learned that the man with the army could do anything.
