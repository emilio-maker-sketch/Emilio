# CHAPTER TWO: THE YOUNG CAESAR

The Julii were one of the oldest families in Rome. They claimed descent from the goddess Venus, through Aeneas, the Trojan hero, and his son Iulus. But by the time Gaius Julius Caesar was born, in July of the year 100 BC, the family had produced few consuls for generations. They lived not on the Palatine with the great houses, but in the Subura, a crowded, noisy working district at the foot of the hills.

The family's politics were set by marriage. Caesar's aunt Julia was the wife of Gaius Marius, the great general. And at about seventeen, Caesar married Cornelia, the daughter of Marius's ally Cinna. When Sulla won the civil war, that made him an enemy. Sulla ordered him to divorce her. Caesar refused.

He lost his priesthood and his wife's dowry, and he had to go into hiding, moving from house to house in the Sabine country, sick with fever, bribing the patrols that hunted him. In the end, relatives and the Vestal Virgins pleaded for him, and Sulla gave way. But, according to Suetonius, he warned them that the boy they were saving would one day destroy them all. In this Caesar, he said, there are many Mariuses.

Then came the story that the Romans loved to tell about him. In his mid-twenties, sailing to the island of Rhodes to study rhetoric, he was captured by pirates. When they told him his ransom would be twenty talents, he laughed at them, and told them he was worth at least fifty.

For more than a month he lived among them while his friends raised the money. He joined their games, read them his poems and speeches, called them illiterate barbarians when they didn't applaud, and promised, cheerfully, that he would come back and crucify them all. They thought it was a joke. When he was freed, he raised a small fleet, captured them and did exactly what he had promised.

The details come from Plutarch and Suetonius, writing a century and a half later, and the story was surely polished in the telling. But it captures something every source agrees on. Caesar was charming, fearless, and absolutely certain of his own destiny.

He was also hugely in debt. To win votes, he borrowed fortunes and spent them on games, gladiators and public feasts. In 63 BC, he stood for the post of Pontifex Maximus, chief priest of Rome, against two of the most senior men in the Senate. That morning, he told his mother that he would come home either as high priest or not at all. He won.

As Pontifex Maximus he moved into the Domus Publica, the official house on the Sacred Way, in the very heart of Rome. It was the house he would leave on the morning of the Ides of March, nearly twenty years later.
