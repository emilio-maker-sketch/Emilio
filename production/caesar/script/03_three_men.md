# CHAPTER THREE: THREE MEN AND GAUL

Caesar's rise needed allies, and in 60 BC he found two. Gnaeus Pompeius, Pompey the Great, was the most famous general in Rome, fresh from conquering the East, and furious that the Senate was refusing to reward his veterans. Marcus Licinius Crassus was the richest man in Rome, and Caesar's biggest creditor. The two men loathed each other. Caesar persuaded them to work together.

Modern historians call their pact the First Triumvirate. At the time it was simply a private deal between three men to share out the Republic. With their money and votes behind him, Caesar was elected consul for 59 BC. His colleague Bibulus tried to block his laws, was driven from the Forum with a basket of dung tipped over his head, and stayed home for the rest of the year. Romans joked that it was the consulship of Julius and Caesar.

To seal the alliance, Pompey married Caesar's daughter, Julia. By every account, it became a real love match. And Caesar got what he wanted most: command of the provinces of Gaul for five years, later extended to ten. He was forty-one, with debts that only a war could pay. Gaul would provide it.

The Gallic War lasted eight years. Caesar fought the Helvetii, the Germans of Ariovistus, the Belgae, and the Veneti on the Atlantic coast. He bridged the Rhine in ten days and twice crossed the sea to Britain, a land most Romans thought lay at the edge of the world.

In 52 BC the Gauls rose together under a young Arvernian chieftain named Vercingetorix. Caesar trapped him in the hill town of Alesia and built two rings of fortifications around it, one facing in, the other facing out against a relief army. When the siege ended, Vercingetorix rode out and laid down his weapons at Caesar's feet. He was kept prisoner for six years, paraded in Caesar's triumph, and then strangled.

Caesar told the story himself, in his Commentaries, written in plain, clear Latin and in the third person, as if Caesar were someone else. They are brilliant, and they are propaganda. According to Pliny the Elder, Caesar counted more than one million one hundred and ninety thousand enemy dead in his wars. Historians suspect the figure is inflated. But the slaughter and enslavement were enormous, and the profits made Caesar one of the richest men alive.

While he was away, the alliance fell apart. In 54 BC Julia died in childbirth, and the bond between her father and her husband died with her. The next year Crassus led an army against the Parthians in the East and was killed at Carrhae, his legions destroyed. Now there were only two men left. And there was room for only one.
