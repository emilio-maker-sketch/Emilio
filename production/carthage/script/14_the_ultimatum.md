# CHAPTER FOURTEEN: THE ULTIMATUM

In 149 BC, Rome declared war. Utica, Carthage's oldest neighbour, saw what was coming and went over to Rome, giving the Romans a harbour just up the coast. The two consuls of the year sailed from Sicily with, according to Appian, eighty thousand infantry and four thousand cavalry. Carthage had no allies, no fleet, and no army left to speak of. It did the only thing it could think of. It surrendered.

Under Roman custom, a people that surrendered gave themselves completely into the hands of Rome, and trusted to its good faith. The Carthaginian envoys were told that the city would keep its freedom, its laws and its territory, on certain conditions. The first was that they send three hundred children from their noblest families to Sicily, as hostages. They did. Appian describes the mothers clinging to the ships as they sailed, some swimming after them into the sea.

Then the consuls, now camped at Utica, gave the second order. Carthage did not need weapons, since it was under Rome's protection. It must hand them all over. Again, Carthage obeyed. Appian says the Romans received two hundred thousand sets of armour, countless javelins and arrows, and two thousand catapults. Long wagon trains carried them out of the city.

Then the consul Censorinus gave the final order. The people of Carthage must leave their city. The Romans were going to destroy it. The Carthaginians could build a new town wherever they liked, provided it was at least eighty stades, about fifteen kilometres, from the sea. For a city that had lived from the sea for six hundred years, it was a sentence of death.

When the envoys brought the news home, the city went mad. Crowds dragged the councillors who had advised surrender from their seats and killed them. Italians who happened to be in the city were murdered. People ran to the empty arsenals, to the temples, to the ship sheds where there were no ships. Then, in desperation, Carthage decided to fight. It closed its gates. It freed its slaves to serve as soldiers. And it recalled a general it had condemned to death only months before, Hasdrubal, who still had an army in the countryside.

The city became one great workshop. Appian says that every day the Carthaginians made one hundred shields, three hundred swords, a thousand catapult bolts, and five hundred spears and javelins. Temples and public buildings were turned into forges, men and women working day and night. There was not enough rope for the catapults. So, Appian says, the women of Carthage cut off their hair and gave it to be twisted into cords.
