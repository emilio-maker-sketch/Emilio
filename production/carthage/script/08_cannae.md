# CHAPTER EIGHT: CANNAE

By 216 BC, Rome had lost patience with delay. The voters elected two new consuls, the cautious aristocrat Lucius Aemilius Paullus and the popular Gaius Terentius Varro, and gave them the largest army Rome had ever put into the field. Eight legions, each one enlarged, with as many again from the Italian allies. Polybius gives a total of eighty thousand infantry and more than six thousand cavalry. Their orders were to find Hannibal and destroy him.

They found him in Apulia, in the south-east of Italy, near a small hilltop town called Cannae, beside the river Aufidus. Hannibal had perhaps forty thousand infantry and ten thousand cavalry. He was outnumbered almost two to one. On the second of August, by the Roman calendar, the two armies formed up on the flat ground by the river.

The Romans planned to smash through the centre with sheer weight of numbers. Hannibal read their plan, and used it against them. In the centre he placed his Gauls and Spaniards, the least reliable troops, in a line that bulged forward toward the enemy like a crescent. On either side, held back, stood his best African infantry. On the wings, his cavalry.

The battle began on the wings. On the riverside, Hannibal's heavy cavalry under Hasdrubal crushed the Roman horsemen, then rode all the way around behind the Roman army to attack the allied cavalry on the far wing, and sent them fleeing too. Meanwhile the Roman infantry pushed forward into the centre. The Gauls and Spaniards gave ground, slowly, step by step, exactly as planned. The crescent bent backward. The Romans pressed into it, thinking they were winning.

Then Hannibal sprang the trap. The African infantry on either side turned inward and attacked the flanks of the Roman mass. The Carthaginian cavalry came back from the pursuit and fell on the Roman rear. The Roman army, still pushing forward, found itself surrounded on every side, packed so tightly that many men could not raise their arms to fight.

What followed was not a battle but a slaughter that lasted the rest of the day. Polybius says seventy thousand Romans died. Livy gives a lower figure, about forty-eight thousand. Either way, it was one of the bloodiest single days of fighting in history. The consul Paullus was killed. So were two former consuls and some eighty senators. Hannibal lost about six thousand men, most of them Gauls.

Livy tells us that Hannibal's brother Mago later stood before the council in Carthage and poured out the gold rings taken from the fingers of dead Roman nobles: a heap so large that, by some accounts, it filled three and a half bushels. More than two thousand years later, armies still study Cannae. The double envelopment, the perfect encirclement of a larger force, is still often called simply, a Cannae.
