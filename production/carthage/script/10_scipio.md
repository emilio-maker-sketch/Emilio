# CHAPTER TEN: SCIPIO

In Spain, the war had gone badly for Rome. In 211 BC, both Roman commanders there, the brothers Publius and Gnaeus Scipio, were killed within weeks of each other. Rome needed someone to take their place. The man who stepped forward was the son who had saved his father's life at the Ticinus seven years before, Publius Cornelius Scipio. He was about twenty-five, and he had never held high office. The people gave him the command anyway.

His first move was the boldest of the war. In 209 BC he marched his army in seven days straight to New Carthage, the enemy's capital in Spain, while the three Carthaginian armies there were far away. The city stood on a peninsula with a lagoon behind it. Scipio had learned from fishermen that the lagoon became shallow enough to wade at certain times of day. While his main army attacked the walls from the front, a detachment waded across the lagoon and climbed the undefended walls on the other side.

New Carthage fell in a single day, with its treasury, its arsenals, its ships, and the hostages Carthage had taken from the Spanish tribes. Scipio sent the hostages home. Polybius tells a famous story: his soldiers brought him a beautiful young captive, and he returned her, untouched, to her fiancé, a Spanish prince. The story was told for centuries as an example of noble restraint, and painted over and over again.

Over the next three years Scipio drove the Carthaginians out of Spain altogether, defeating them at Baecula and at Ilipa. Then he went home, was elected consul, and argued for a plan the old Fabius thought insane. Instead of chasing Hannibal around Italy, Rome should invade Africa, and force Carthage to call him home.

In 204 BC, Scipio landed in Africa near Utica. He found a vital ally there: Masinissa, a young Numidian prince who had fought against Rome in Spain, had been driven from his kingdom, and now changed sides. His horsemen, who rode without saddle or bridle, were the best light cavalry in the world.

In the spring of 203, Scipio struck by night. The enemy armies of Carthage and its Numidian allies were camped in huts made of reeds and branches. His men set them on fire, and cut down the soldiers as they ran out. Polybius calls it the most dazzling of all Scipio's exploits. A few weeks later he won a pitched battle on the Great Plains.

Carthage did what Scipio had expected. It called Hannibal home. In the autumn of 203 BC, after fifteen years in Italy, he sailed for Africa. Livy says that few exiles ever left their own country as sadly as Hannibal left the country of his enemies.
