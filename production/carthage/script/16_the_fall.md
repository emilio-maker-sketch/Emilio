# CHAPTER SIXTEEN: THE LAST DAYS

In the spring of 146 BC, Scipio attacked. His men broke in by the harbours. Scipio's friend Gaius Laelius took the wall around the circular naval harbour, and the Romans poured into the great public square of Carthage, its agora, near the sea. Starving and exhausted, the defenders fell back. That night, Roman soldiers stripped the gold from a temple of Apollo, as the Greeks called the Phoenician god Reshef, hacking it off with their swords.

From the agora, three narrow streets climbed the hill to the Byrsa, the citadel at the heart of the city. They were lined with houses six storeys high. This is where the fighting of our opening scene took place. For six days and nights, the Romans fought their way up, house by house, crossing from roof to roof on planks and beams. Then, to clear a path for the troops, Scipio ordered the streets set on fire.

The excavations on the Byrsa hill in the 1970s and 1980s, led by the French archaeologist Serge Lancel, found exactly this: a district of tall houses built in the second century BC, ending in a thick layer of ash, burnt timber, fallen roof tiles and sling stones.

On the seventh day, the people on the Byrsa sent envoys down to ask that those who came out would be allowed to live. Scipio agreed, except for deserters. Fifty thousand men and women came out of the citadel. Nine hundred Roman deserters, who knew they would be shown no mercy, took refuge in the temple of Eshmun, the god of healing, at the very summit of the hill. With them were Hasdrubal, his wife and their two sons.

Then Hasdrubal broke. He slipped out alone, carrying an olive branch, and threw himself at Scipio's feet. Scipio had him displayed to the deserters on the temple roof. And on the roof, in front of everyone, Hasdrubal's wife appeared, in her finest clothes, holding her two sons. Polybius, who was there, records that she called out to her husband as a traitor and a coward. Then she killed her children, threw them into the burning temple, and followed them into the flames.

Later writers said Carthage burned for seventeen days. As Scipio watched, Polybius saw him weep. He was thinking, he said, of how cities and empires rise and fall, as Troy had fallen, and Assyria, and Persia, and Macedon. Then he quoted the lines of Homer in which a Trojan hero foresees the fall of his own city. When Polybius asked what he meant, Scipio took him by the hand. He feared, he said, that one day someone would say the same about Rome.
