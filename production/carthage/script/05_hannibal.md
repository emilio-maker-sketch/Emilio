# CHAPTER FIVE: HANNIBAL

We do not know what Hannibal looked like. No portrait of him survives that we can be sure of. Some silver coins minted by his family in Spain show a young beardless man and an older bearded one, and some historians think these are Barca family portraits. A marble bust in Naples has been called Hannibal since the Renaissance, but it is a guess.

What we do have are descriptions written by his enemies. The Roman historian Livy, writing two centuries later, says that no man was ever better at both obeying and commanding. He endured heat and cold alike. He ate and slept when there was time, not when it was night. He was often seen asleep on the ground among the sentries, wrapped in a common soldier's cloak. He was the first into battle and the last to leave it.

Livy also gave him a list of vices: inhuman cruelty, and more than Punic treachery. That phrase, Punic faith, became a Roman proverb for lying. We should remember who was writing.

Hannibal spent two years strengthening his hold on Spain. Then he turned to Saguntum, a wealthy town on the coast south of the Ebro, which had placed itself under Rome's protection. In 219 BC he laid siege to it. The Saguntines held out for eight months, expecting Roman help that never came. When the city fell, many of its people, according to Livy, burned themselves and their possessions rather than surrender.

In Rome, the news caused outrage, and an embassy sailed to Carthage. Its leader, Quintus Fabius, stood before the Carthaginian council and gathered a fold of his toga in his hand. Here, he said, we bring you peace and war. Choose which you please. Give us whichever you like, the Carthaginians shouted back. Then I give you war, he said, and let the fold fall. They answered that they accepted it.

Rome expected to fight the war on its own terms. Its fleet ruled the sea. One consul, Publius Cornelius Scipio, would sail to Spain to deal with Hannibal. The other would invade Africa. But Hannibal had a different plan. If he could not reach Italy by sea, he would go by land, across the Pyrenees, across the Rhône, and over the Alps, and fight the Romans in their own country, where their allies might abandon them.
