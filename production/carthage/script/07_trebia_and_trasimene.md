# CHAPTER SEVEN: THE TRAP IN THE FOG

The Gallic tribes of northern Italy, who had recently been conquered by Rome, began to come over to Hannibal. Scipio, back from the Rhône, met him first in a cavalry clash near the river Ticinus. The Romans were beaten and the consul was badly wounded. According to the story later told by his friend Laelius, he was saved by his seventeen-year-old son, who charged into the enemy alone to rescue him. The boy was also named Publius Cornelius Scipio. Remember him.

In December, at the river Trebia, Hannibal set his first great trap. Before dawn, on a freezing morning, he sent his Numidian cavalry to ride up to the Roman camp and taunt the enemy. The second consul, Sempronius, eager for glory, led his army out without breakfast, through the icy river swollen to chest height, and into battle soaked and starving. Hannibal's men had eaten, rested and rubbed themselves with oil around their fires.

As the battle reached its height, two thousand men under Hannibal's brother Mago burst from a hidden streambed behind the Roman lines. The Roman army broke. About ten thousand Romans fought their way through the centre of the enemy line and escaped. Most of the rest were killed, captured, or drowned trying to recross the river.

That winter almost all of Hannibal's elephants died of the cold. In the spring of 217 BC he took the army across the flooded marshes of the river Arno. For four days and three nights the men marched through water. Many pack animals collapsed and died, and the soldiers slept on their carcasses to keep out of the water. Hannibal, riding the last surviving elephant, caught an infection that cost him the sight of one eye.

The new consul, Gaius Flaminius, followed him south. Near Lake Trasimene, the road ran along a narrow strip between the water and a line of hills. Hannibal hid his entire army on the hills overnight. In the morning a thick mist rose off the lake. The Roman column marched into the valley without scouting it.

At a signal, Hannibal's men charged down out of the fog from every direction. The Romans could not see the enemy, could not form up, could not run. Livy says the fighting was so fierce that no one noticed an earthquake. Flaminius was killed by a Gallic horseman. In three hours, about fifteen thousand Romans died. Thousands more drowned trying to swim out into the lake.

In Rome, a magistrate stood up before the crowd in the Forum and announced simply: We have been defeated in a great battle. The Senate turned to an old aristocrat, Quintus Fabius Maximus, and made him dictator. Fabius had a new idea. He would not fight Hannibal at all.

He shadowed the Carthaginian army along the hills, cut off its foraging parties and starved it, but refused every invitation to battle. The Romans hated it. They called him Cunctator, the Delayer, and laughed at him as Hannibal's servant. Once, Fabius trapped Hannibal in a valley in Campania. That night Hannibal had burning torches tied to the horns of two thousand oxen, and drove them up a hillside. The Romans rushed to block what they thought was the army escaping, and Hannibal slipped through the pass behind them.
