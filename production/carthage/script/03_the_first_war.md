# CHAPTER THREE: THE WAR FOR SICILY

In 264 BC, a band of mercenaries who had seized the Sicilian city of Messana found themselves under attack, and appealed for help to both Carthage and Rome. Rome had by now conquered most of Italy. After a fierce debate, the Romans decided to send an army across the narrow strait into Sicily. It was the first time a Roman army had ever left Italy. It began the first of three wars that the Romans called Punic, from their word for Phoenician.

Carthage was a naval power. Rome was not. The Romans had almost no warships, and none of the heavy galleys called quinqueremes that dominated the sea. According to Polybius, they found a Carthaginian quinquereme that had run aground, used it as a model, and built a hundred more in sixty days, training their oarsmen on benches set up on dry land while the ships were being finished.

To make up for their lack of skill, the Romans invented a boarding bridge with a heavy iron spike on the end, which they called the corvus, the raven. When an enemy ship came close, the bridge dropped, the spike drove into the deck, and Roman soldiers ran across to fight as if they were on land. At Mylae in 260 BC the Carthaginian fleet was taken completely by surprise.

The war dragged on for twenty-three years. In 256 BC, after one of the largest sea battles in history, the Romans landed an army in Africa itself under the consul Marcus Atilius Regulus. Carthage seemed about to fall. But the Carthaginians hired a Spartan mercenary, Xanthippus, who used their elephants and cavalry to smash the Roman army on open ground. Regulus was captured.

Then the sea itself turned against Rome. In 255 BC, a Roman fleet ignored the warnings of its pilots and was caught by a storm off the south coast of Sicily. According to Polybius, of three hundred and sixty-four ships, only eighty survived. Tens of thousands of men drowned. Even so, the Romans simply built another fleet.

In Sicily the war ground on. A Carthaginian general named Hamilcar, nicknamed Barca, probably meaning lightning, held out for years on a mountain near Panormus, modern Palermo, and later at Eryx, raiding the Italian coast and never losing a battle. Remember his name. His family would shape the next fifty years.

In 241 BC, with its treasury empty, the Roman Senate accepted loans from private citizens to build one last fleet of two hundred ships. Off the Aegates Islands, west of Sicily, it caught a Carthaginian supply fleet heavily loaded and destroyed it. Since 2004, divers have found more than twenty bronze warship rams from this battle on the seabed, some still inscribed with the names of the officials who commissioned them.

Carthage asked for peace. It gave up Sicily, which became Rome's first overseas province, and agreed to pay a huge indemnity of three thousand two hundred talents of silver over ten years. Hamilcar came down from his mountain undefeated, and never forgave the men who had made him surrender.
