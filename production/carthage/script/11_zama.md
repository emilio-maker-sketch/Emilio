# CHAPTER ELEVEN: ZAMA

In 202 BC, the two greatest generals of their age faced each other on a plain near a town called Zama, south-west of Carthage. Polybius says that before the battle they met in person, between the armies, each with an interpreter. Hannibal offered peace. Scipio said it was too late. Whether they really spoke, or whether the meeting was invented by later writers, we cannot be sure.

Hannibal opened the battle with his eighty war elephants. Scipio was ready. He had arranged his infantry not in a solid line, but in columns with open lanes between them. As the elephants charged, the Romans blew trumpets and horns. Many of the terrified animals ran down the lanes and out the back without hurting anyone. Others turned around and stampeded into Hannibal's own cavalry.

Scipio's cavalry, the Italians under his friend Laelius and the Numidians under Masinissa, drove the Carthaginian horsemen off the field and chased them away. Then the infantry met. Hannibal's first two lines were broken. But his third line, his veterans from Italy, held firm, and for a long time the outcome hung in the balance.

Then Scipio's cavalry returned, and charged into the back of Hannibal's line. It was Cannae in reverse. Polybius says twenty thousand Carthaginians were killed, and almost as many captured. Hannibal escaped with a handful of horsemen. For the first time in his life, he had lost a great battle.

Back in Carthage, a councillor stood up to argue against accepting Rome's terms. Hannibal walked over and pulled him down from the platform. Then he told the astonished councillors that he had left Carthage as a boy of nine, and had come back at forty-five, and that he knew more about war than about the customs of their assembly. Carthage must make peace.

The terms of 201 BC were harsh. Carthage would keep its lands in Africa but lose everything overseas. It had to hand over all its elephants and all but ten of its warships. It had to pay ten thousand talents of silver over fifty years. And it could not make war on anyone, even in Africa, without Rome's permission. Livy says the Romans towed some five hundred Carthaginian ships out to sea, and burned them in sight of the city.

Scipio returned to Rome in triumph, and took the name by which he is still known: Africanus. Hannibal, against all expectation, stayed in Carthage. He was not finished yet.
