# CHAPTER ONE: THE NEW CITY

The story begins far to the east, on the coast of what is now Lebanon. Here, three thousand years ago, stood the cities of the Phoenicians: Byblos, Sidon, and above all Tyre, built on an island just off the shore. The Phoenicians were the great sailors and traders of their age. They sold cedar wood, glass, and a purple dye so costly that it became the colour of kings.

They also spread something even more valuable: their alphabet. The Greeks borrowed it, and from the Greek alphabet came the Latin letters you are reading on this screen. Phoenician ships pushed further and further west, past Cyprus, Sicily and Sardinia, all the way through the Strait of Gibraltar, founding trading posts along the way.

One of those settlements, on a peninsula in what is now Tunisia, was given a simple name. Qart-hadasht. The New City. The Greeks called it Karchedon. The Romans called it Carthago. We call it Carthage.

According to the legend, the city was founded by a princess of Tyre named Elissa, whom the Romans would call Dido. Her brother, King Pygmalion, had murdered her husband for his wealth. Elissa fled with her followers and the treasure, sailed west, and landed on the African shore.

She asked the local ruler for as much land as could be covered by the hide of an ox. He agreed. So she cut the hide into strips so thin that, laid end to end, they circled a whole hill. That hill became the citadel of Carthage, the Byrsa. In Greek, byrsa means ox-hide. It is a good story, but it is almost certainly a pun on a Phoenician word for a fortress.

Roman writers added a love story. In Virgil's Aeneid, Dido falls in love with the Trojan hero Aeneas, and when he abandons her to found Rome she kills herself, cursing his descendants with eternal enmity. It is pure poetry, written centuries later, but it tells you how the Romans came to see Carthage: as the enemy destined from the beginning.

The ancient tradition dated the founding to 814 BC. For a long time historians doubted it. But the oldest objects found in the city date from the late ninth or eighth century BC, close enough to suggest the tradition was not far wrong. Carthage was older than Rome itself.
