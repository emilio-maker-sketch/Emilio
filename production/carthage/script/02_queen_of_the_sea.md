# CHAPTER TWO: QUEEN OF THE SEA

When Tyre itself fell under the power of the great empires of the East, Carthage became the leader of the Phoenician West. By the fourth century BC it controlled the coast of North Africa, western Sicily, Sardinia, the Balearic Islands and stretches of southern Spain. Its fleets kept rival traders out. Its merchants bought tin, silver, grain and slaves, and sold them across the Mediterranean.

The city itself grew into one of the largest in the Mediterranean world. Its houses climbed the slopes of the Byrsa, with tall apartment blocks on narrow streets, and grand villas with gardens on the outskirts. Water was precious, so almost every house had its own cistern. Archaeologists have found bathtubs, mosaic floors and drains that would not look out of place in a Roman town centuries later.

Most famous of all were the harbours. A ship entering from the sea passed first into a long rectangular basin for merchant ships. A channel led from there into a second, circular harbour reserved for the navy. In its centre was an island with a tower, from which the admiral could watch the open sea. All around the edge of the basin, and on the island, were covered sheds for some two hundred and twenty warships, each fronted by a pair of columns, so that the whole harbour looked like one great colonnade.

Carthage was a republic, like Rome. Each year it elected two chief magistrates, called suffetes. There was a council of elders and a popular assembly, and a court of one hundred and four judges who watched over the generals. A general who lost a battle could be put on trial, and sometimes crucified. The Greek philosopher Aristotle admired the Carthaginian constitution and praised its stability.

Its people worshipped Phoenician gods: Baal Hammon, and a goddess called Tanit, whose sign, a triangle with a circle above it, is carved on thousands of stones. Some of those stones stand in a sacred precinct that archaeologists call the tophet. Under them were buried urns holding the cremated bones of infants and young animals.

Greek and Roman writers said that the Carthaginians sacrificed their own children to their gods in times of crisis. Some modern scholars have argued that this was enemy propaganda, and that the tophet was simply a cemetery for children who died young. The debate goes on, but many archaeologists now accept that sacrifices did happen, at least at times. It is worth remembering that almost every word we have about Carthage was written by its enemies.

For centuries Carthage fought the Greek cities of Sicily, above all Syracuse. With Rome, a small city in central Italy, it was on friendly terms. The Greek historian Polybius copied out the text of a treaty between the two cities made in the first year of the Roman Republic. Rome was to stay out of Carthage's waters. Carthage was to leave Rome's Latin allies alone. For two hundred and fifty years, it worked.
