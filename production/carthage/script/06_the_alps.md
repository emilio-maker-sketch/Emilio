# CHAPTER SIX: OVER THE ALPS

In the late spring of 218 BC, Hannibal marched out of New Carthage. Polybius gives him ninety thousand infantry, twelve thousand cavalry, and thirty-seven war elephants. The starting numbers may be exaggerated, but it was a huge force. He crossed the Ebro, fought his way through the tribes north of it, and climbed over the Pyrenees, leaving troops behind to hold what he had won.

At the Rhône, a Gallic tribe waited on the far bank to stop him. Hannibal sent part of his army upstream by night, under an officer named Hanno, to cross in secret and fall on the Gauls from behind. The elephants were a problem. They refused to step onto boats. So the Carthaginians built huge rafts, covered them with earth so that they looked like solid ground, and coaxed the animals onto them with two females leading the way.

Days later, a Roman army under the consul Scipio landed at the mouth of the Rhône, on its way to Spain. It arrived to find that Hannibal had already gone, three days earlier, heading inland. Scipio made a remarkable decision. He sent his army on to Spain under his brother, to cut Hannibal off from his base, and sailed back to Italy alone to meet him there.

It was autumn when Hannibal reached the mountains. Mountain tribes attacked his long column in narrow passes, rolling rocks down onto men and pack animals. Horses and mules slipped and fell from the paths. At the top of the pass, snow was already falling. Polybius says that Hannibal gathered his exhausted men and pointed out the plains of Italy, far below, to give them heart.

The way down was worse. A landslide had swept away the path. Livy says the soldiers split a great rock by heating it with fire and pouring vinegar on it. Polybius, who walked the route himself to check it, does not mention this. The whole crossing took about fifteen days.

Which pass did he use? Nobody knows for certain. Historians have argued for at least half a dozen routes. In 2016, scientists studying a high pass called the Col de la Traversette reported a layer of churned-up earth full of horse dung dating to around this time. It is an intriguing clue, but not proof.

When Hannibal came down into the valley of the Po, he had twenty thousand infantry and six thousand cavalry left. We know these numbers because he later had them carved on a bronze tablet in southern Italy, where Polybius read them. He had crossed the Pyrenees with about fifty-nine thousand men. More than half of them were gone. But he was in Italy.
