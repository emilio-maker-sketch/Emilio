# CHAPTER THIRTEEN: CARTHAGE MUST BE DESTROYED

Rome's ally in Africa was the Numidian king Masinissa, who had fought beside Scipio at Zama. He was a remarkable man. Polybius met him when he was nearly ninety and says he could still ride all day, and stand in one place for hours. He had made his nomadic people into farmers, and turned Numidia into a powerful kingdom.

The treaty of 201 BC said Carthage could not go to war, even in Africa, without Rome's permission. Masinissa used this ruthlessly. Again and again he seized pieces of Carthaginian territory. Again and again Carthage complained to Rome. Roman commissions came, looked, and either sided with Masinissa or went home without deciding anything.

Around 153 BC, one of those commissions included an old Roman senator named Marcus Porcius Cato. Cato had fought in the war against Hannibal as a young man. He was famous for his harshness, his thrift and his hatred of luxury. What he saw at Carthage shocked him. The city was not ruined and humbled. It was rich, crowded and full of young men.

Back in Rome, Plutarch tells us, he let some figs fall from the fold of his toga in the Senate. While the senators admired how large and fresh they were, he told them that the country where they grew was only three days' sail from Rome.

From then on, whatever the subject of the debate, Cato ended every speech in the Senate with the same words. Carthage must be destroyed. The famous Latin phrase, Carthago delenda est, is a later summary, but the idea is his. Another senator, Scipio Nasica, always answered the opposite: Carthage should be spared. Rome, he argued, needed a rival to fear, to keep it disciplined.

In 151 BC, Carthage made the last of its fifty annual payments. In its own eyes it was free. Soon afterwards, after yet another attack by Masinissa, it raised an army of its own and marched out to fight him. It was beaten, surrounded and starved, and most of the army was killed. A young Roman officer who happened to be visiting Masinissa watched the battle from a hill. His name was Scipio Aemilianus.

He was the son of the Roman general Aemilius Paullus, adopted into the family of the Scipios: the grandson, by adoption, of Scipio Africanus. And the battle he had just watched gave Rome what Cato had been waiting for. Carthage had made war without Rome's permission. It had broken the treaty.
