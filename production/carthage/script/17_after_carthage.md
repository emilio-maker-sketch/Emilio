# CHAPTER SEVENTEEN: AFTER CARTHAGE

The Senate sent ten commissioners to decide what should be done. Whatever was left of Carthage was to be razed to the ground, and a curse was laid upon the site. No one was ever to live there again. The libraries were given away to the Numidian kings. Its territory became a new Roman province, called simply Africa. In the same year, on the other side of the Mediterranean, another Roman army sacked the great Greek city of Corinth. 146 BC was the year Rome became the unchallenged master of the Mediterranean world.

You may have heard that the Romans ploughed the ground and sowed it with salt, so that nothing would ever grow there again. There is no ancient source for this. The story first appears in modern books, in the twentieth century. It is a myth.

The curse did not last long. A generation later, the reformer Gaius Gracchus tried to found a Roman colony on the site. Julius Caesar planned another, and his heir Augustus built it. Roman Carthage became one of the greatest cities of the empire, the capital of Africa, with vast public baths, a circus, an amphitheatre and theatres, its harbours rebuilt on the old Punic plan. It was in Roman Carthage, centuries later, that the young Augustine studied.

The Phoenician language, Punic, lived on too. It was still spoken in the countryside of North Africa six hundred years after the fall, in the time of Augustine. Carthage itself passed to the Vandals, then the Byzantines, and was finally abandoned after the Arab conquest at the end of the seventh century, its stones carried off for centuries to build Tunis and other cities.

Today the site of Carthage is a quiet suburb of Tunis, with villas, gardens and a presidential palace. Since 1979 it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site. You can still walk around the circular harbour, now a lagoon, with its small round island where the admiral's tower once stood. On the Byrsa hill, the houses that burned in 146 BC have been uncovered again. And in the tophet, the little stones of Tanit still stand in the grass.

We know Carthage almost entirely through the eyes of its enemies: Polybius, the friend of the man who destroyed it; Livy, who wrote Rome's national epic; and Appian, two centuries later still. Carthage's own books are gone. Its own version of its story is lost. We will never hear its side.

Cato died in 149 BC, before he could see the war he had demanded won. Scipio Aemilianus, who had wept for Rome as he burned Carthage, was found dead in his bed twenty years later, during the political violence that would eventually tear the Roman Republic apart. And Hannibal, who never captured Rome, became more famous than almost any Roman of his age. Generals have studied his campaigns ever since.

For generations, Romans used the words Hannibal at the gates to mean mortal danger. They never forgot that, for fifteen years, one man from a city they destroyed had held Rome itself in the balance. Carthage had been the New City. Rome made sure it would also be the last city of its kind. And Scipio's fear, at least, came true. Six centuries later, Rome itself would be sacked, and its empire in the west would fall.
