# CHAPTER TWELVE: THE EXILE

In 196 BC, Hannibal was elected suffete, one of the two chief magistrates of Carthage. He went after the corrupt officials who had grown rich while the state struggled to pay Rome. He made the powerful court of judges, whose members had held office for life, subject to election every year. He found that so much public money was being stolen that, once the theft stopped, the indemnity could be paid without any new taxes.

He made powerful enemies, and they sent word to Rome that Hannibal was plotting with King Antiochus the Third, the ruler of the vast Seleucid empire in the East. A Roman commission arrived. In 195 BC, Hannibal slipped out of the city at night, rode to the coast, and sailed for Tyre, the ancient mother city of Carthage. He would never see Africa again.

Meanwhile, Carthage did something unexpected. It got rich again. Freed from the cost of empire and war, its farms and its trade flourished. In 191 BC it offered to pay off the entire rest of the fifty-year indemnity at once. Rome refused. The payments were not really about money. They were a reminder of who was in charge.

Carthaginian agriculture was so admired that, after the city's fall, the Roman Senate ordered a Carthaginian farming manual, by a writer named Mago, to be translated into Latin. It was the only book from the libraries of Carthage that Rome considered worth keeping. And archaeologists have found something even more surprising. The famous circular naval harbour, in the form described by the ancient writers, seems to have been built in this period, after the war with Hannibal, even though Carthage was allowed only ten warships.

Hannibal served Antiochus as an adviser and admiral, but the king ignored most of his advice and was crushed by the Romans in 190 BC. The peace treaty demanded that Hannibal be handed over. He fled again, and found shelter at last with King Prusias of Bithynia, in what is now northern Turkey. There, according to the Roman writer Nepos, he won a sea battle for his host by having jars full of poisonous snakes thrown onto the enemy's ships.

Around 183 BC, the Romans sent an envoy to demand that Prusias surrender him. Soldiers surrounded Hannibal's house. He had long kept poison ready, and now he took it. Livy gives his last words. Let us free the Romans from their long anxiety, since they think it too long to wait for the death of an old man. He was about sixty-four.

In about the same year, Scipio Africanus died too, bitter and out of favour, at his country estate. It was said that he asked to be buried there, not in Rome. The two great enemies left the stage together. But the story of Carthage was not over.
